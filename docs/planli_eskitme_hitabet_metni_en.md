# THE LEGAL, RATIONAL, AND MORAL RULING ON PLANNED OBSOLESCENCE

Today, I stand before you to address an economic injustice that has entrenched itself in the heart of modern commerce: planned obsolescence and the apparatus of platform captivity.

I will not begin by pointing to specific consumer gadgets. Mention a particular battery, an interchangeable circuit board, or an encrypted software update, and our discourse is immediately hijacked by the peculiar physics and transient regulations of that single artifact. The universal principle is lost. We must proceed as classical jurists have always proceeded: through *tenkîh al-manât*—the deliberate isolation of the operative legal ground. We strip away the corporate trademark, the product category, and the transient hardware shell. What remains is the naked reality of the transaction: an economic architecture governed by asymmetric power, strict delictual liability, and objective normative variables. Every argument I present rests squarely upon this foundation.

Look now at the first panel on your screen, which charts our universal jurisprudential model. 

In any manufacturing ecosystem, four distinct entities hold legal standing. First is the maker, *es-Sâni*, who commands asymmetric knowledge and architectural control. Second is the distributor or vendor, *el-Bâi*, who transfers physical custody at the moment of exchange, *t0*. Third is the purchaser, *el-Mâlik*, who pays the full commutative value *Semen* to acquire absolute proprietary title, *Milk Tamm*, thereby shouldering the natural temporal risk of wear. And fourth is the public boundary—*Hukukullâh*—the thermodynamic carrying capacity of our shared planetary commons (*MüştereklerEşiği*), an ecological and social ceiling that no private contract may lawfully breach.

Alongside these actors stand the governing structural parameters. There is the durable asset itself, *Metâ*. There is the purchase price, *Semen*. There is *AsliKapasite*—the inherent durability benchmark woven into the physical metallurgy, chemical constitution, and logical code of the good. And set against it is *KasıtlıKısıt*—an artificial, engineered ceiling of premature failure intentionally designed to terminate the good far short of its natural potential. To test whether any design choice is legitimate, we apply the strict proportionality filter of Article 22 of the Mecelle: the technical gain achieved must genuinely balance the utility destroyed. Where that proportion nears zero, claims of technical necessity (*TeknikZaruret*) collapse into an unlawful abuse of rights (*sû-i isti'mâl-i hak*). When platform lock-in (*EsaretKatsayısı*) nears unity, market discipline breaks down entirely, allowing the maker to extract full price for truncated lifespans. And whenever the cumulative waste dumped into the environment breaches the carrying capacity of the commons, all exculpatory contractual disclaimers become void ab initio under Article 26 of the Mecelle.

Look at how this trap is laid across the market. At the factory gate, the buyer pays full market value *Semen* expecting a sound, enduring product *AsliKapasite*, but receives a covertly hobbled asset *KasıtlıKısıt*. In plain juristic terms, that is *ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl*—unjust enrichment condemned in Surah An-Nisâ. Then consider the assembly: when a minor battery degrades, the entire machine must be thrown away because repair is blocked. Worse still, long after title has passed, the maker reaches across the network into the owner's home and arbitrarily throttles performance. That is direct, actionable trespass—*mübâşereten itlâf* under Mecelle Article 92. Add to this the cryptographic locks that outlaw third-party repair, creating artificial scarcity, and you have classic *ihtikâr* and coercive *bay'u'l-mudtarr*.

Before we examine each model in detail, we must clear the field of six persistent analytical confusions.

First, we must distinguish between an actionable latent defect and natural material fatigue. In materials science, components wear out at different rates. Classical jurisprudence never demands the impossible: it does not compare the lifespan of a fragile sub-component against a steel frame. What the law condemns is not natural physical entropy, but the deliberate suppression of intrinsic potential. When a manufacturer designs an unserviceable monolithic block solely to prevent the renewal of a depleted cell, or embeds a hidden software counter that kills the machine, that is an affirmative, actionable wrong—an *ayb-i hafî* born of active concealment (*taghrîr*).

Second, consider the apparent tension between our first and eighth evidences. How can we say the buyer pays full price expecting a sound product, while also demonstrating that the entire market collapses into a lemons equilibrium where everyone expects low quality? The answer lies in time and market power. At the micro-contract level, early transactions occur under profound information asymmetry: the maker charges a sound price for defective goods. At the macro level, prolonged deception drives honest producers out of the market. Within platform oligopolies, high switching costs ensure that even when consumers suspect early death, prices never drop. Buyers pay premium prices for disposable machines because they cannot walk away.

Third, look at the profound alignment between circular industrial design and Islamic contract structures. When an asset is sold under an outright sale (*bey'*), full sovereign title passes to the buyer. The seller has no business meddling with the machine post-sale. Conversely, if a company operates a genuine circular lease (*icâre*)—retaining the asset on its own corporate balance sheet—the economic incentive for planned obsolescence vanishes overnight. Every premature failure becomes a direct loss to the maker, making maximum durability and modular repair the primary engines of profit. What the law condemns is the predatory hybrid: charging the upfront capital price of a true sale, while covertly retaining the remote demolition powers of an eternal feudal landlord.

Fourth, observe how our model honors the pure economic loss doctrine. In the law of torts, disappointed commercial expectations arising from an internal product defect are handled through contract, not tort. Our framework respects this divide completely. In Model One, factory-level truncation is treated as a contractual defect and upstream misrepresentation (*al-taghrîr al-fi'lî*), remedied through rescission and cascade indemnification against the supply chain. But in Models Two and Three, the maker crosses the line into delictual trespass (*mübâşereten itlâf*): they reach into property that has already vested in another and intentionally degrade it from afar.

Fifth, remember the foundational maxim of Article 87 of the Mecelle: *Al-kharâju bi'd-damân*—yield follows liability for loss. The buyer who enjoys the fruits of a machine after delivery justly shoulders the natural risks of physical wear, chemical decay, and legitimate technological obsolescence. The manufacturer is not an open-ended insurer against nature. What the maker can never lawfully do is plant secret kill-switches into the machine. The owner accepts the risk of natural aging; the owner never assumes the risk of hidden commercial sabotage.

Sixth, we must reject the illusion of zero-cost security. Closing deep architectural vulnerabilities may impose authentic operational overhead. Classical law recognizes necessity (*darûrah*), but under Article 22 of the Mecelle, necessity is strictly measured by its genuine scope. An authentic, transparent patch that incidentally trims throughput to avert systemic collapse is lawful. What is strictly forbidden is using an unannounced performance penalty as an excuse to cripple the hardware and force the consumer into buying a replacement.

Strip away corporate marketing, and what is planned obsolescence at its core? It is an affirmative wrong committed whenever an engineered ceiling *KasıtlıKısıt*, a digital repair lock, or an ecosystem barrier is imposed without genuine technical proportionality. The manufacturer truncates the baseline life of the good and dumps the resulting ecological debris onto society. How does an adjudicator establish this in practice? You do not need to read the manufacturer's secret thoughts. The procedural standard is objective. The owner or the public authority presents a prima facie case of premature fleet failure or artificial modular lockout. Independent engineering teardowns and audit logs then shift the evidentiary burden straight onto the maker: let them prove that no open, less restrictive design could have preserved functionality.

Three clear boundaries separate honest engineering from commercial fraud. First is natural wear, borne by the owner under *al-kharâju bi'd-damân*. Second is genuine computational obsolescence: when an aging processor simply cannot execute newly developed routines, the maker may cease software expansion while leaving the existing baseline stable and intact. Third is the retirement of external cloud infrastructure, provided the maker does not deliberately brick the local hardware or lock out offline operation. Disclosing a premature limit in unread terms of service changes nothing: fine print may resolve bilateral misrepresentation, but it can never sanitize an ecological crime against the public commons.

Our argument unfolds across two distinct theaters. First, eight inescapable legal proofs grounded in our four analytical models. Second, a systematic refutation of the four primary defenses raised by manufacturers.

Now turn your attention to the second panel on your screen: Model One, the factory phase.

Here, the manufacturer faces an initial design choice. Path A1 embodies the genuine, uncurtailed durability *AsliKapasite* inherent in the physical architecture, allowing weary components to be maintained or replaced without destroying the chassis. Path A2, by contrast, deliberately welds the entire asset to its shortest-lived element, capping its life at *KasıtlıKısıt*. Any claimed cost difference between the two paths is either non-existent or completely trivial when weighed against the massive destruction of operational utility. Bound by ecosystem switching barriers, the consumer pays a sound price *Semen* for an asset secretly hobbled from birth.

Consider our first evidence: commutative disparity, latent defect, and upstream fraud.

In fair commutative exchange, market value reflects intrinsic operational utility. When a maker deliberately curtails that capacity, they introduce an objective defect into commerce. Under informational opacity, the price remains pegged to sound durability. While the law tolerates trivial subjective differences, neutral appraisers quickly recognize that an asset engineered for premature death has lost a major share of its true economic value at the very moment of sale. The resulting gulf between the price paid and the true value constitutes unconscionable disparity (*gabn-i fâhiş*) compounded by active deception (*taghrîr*). As Surah An-Nisâ proclaims: "Do not consume your wealth among yourselves by false ways." Selling a booby-trapped good at full price is the very definition of *ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl*. Under Article 356 of the Mecelle, the purchaser has the right to rescind the contract against the seller. And when the retail vendor pays that restitution, the law grants them full upstream recourse against the manufacturer who designed the deception.

Our second evidence builds upon this: the doctrine of objective latent defect.

A durable asset carries an implied warranty of soundness. The proprietary blueprints containing the artificial limit *KasıtlıKısıt* are known only to the maker; neither the retailer nor the buyer can discover them at the counter. Delivering an artificially stunted good is an act of constructive misrepresentation. The Prophet, peace be upon him, strictly forbade fraudulent and deceptive sales, declaring: "Whoever cheats us is not of us." The wrong does not require proof of malicious internal animus; the objective presence of an engineered bottleneck buried beneath an outward appearance of soundness is an *ayb-i hafî*. Under Article 337 of the Mecelle, the buyer may return the good for a full refund; and where physical restitution is barred by prolonged use, Articles 343 through 345 grant an immediate action for the price difference (*erş*), with liability cascading directly back to the manufacturer.

Our third evidence addresses manufactured captivity, unjust enrichment, and premature gain.

In an open, competitive market, premature failure would drive the customer straight to a rival. To prevent this escape, the maker builds proprietary moats: exclusive cables, non-standard connectors, and locked data formats. When the good fails prematurely, the captive consumer cannot switch without abandoning substantial invested capital. The manufacturer reaps accelerated replacement revenue derived entirely from the buyer's artificial vulnerability. Article 19 of the Mecelle establishes that injury must be eliminated. More crucially, Article 99 codifies a universal principle: *Men iste'cele şey'en kable evânihî ûkıbe bi-hırmânih*—whoever attempts to hasten a benefit by illegitimate means is penalized by total forfeiture. The maker plans early failure to pull forward future sales; the law responds by stripping away that unjust gain through compulsory restitution and disgorgement.

Turn now to the third panel: Model Two, addressing post-sale intervention.

Here, the contract was concluded at time *t0*. Full ownership passed to the buyer. The device operates perfectly. Yet at a later date, *t1*, the manufacturer uses a concealed logic routine, a delayed command, or a background network channel to degrade performance, disable existing features, or induce artificial system rejections.

Our fourth evidence addresses this directly: post-sale trespass, direct destruction, and the betrayal of trust.

Conveyance vests absolute proprietary sovereignty (*Milk Tamm*) in the purchaser, encompassing the unrestricted right to use, modify, calibrate, and repair the chattel. When manufacturers claim that customers clicked "agree" on a digital update, contract law draws an unbending line: consent to routine maintenance can never be construed as authorization for property destruction. In Islamic jurisprudence, custodial access (*amânat al-hifz*) strictly excludes destructive intervention; overstepping that custodial mandate is unauthorized intermeddling (*tasarruf fî milk al-gayr*) and a breach of trust. Under Article 92 of the Mecelle, anyone who directly destroys or damages the property of another is strictly liable to pay full compensation, irrespective of whether they acted with specific malice or claimed administrative convenience. The maker who remotely degrades an asset acts as a direct tortfeasor (*mübâşir*). The owner is entitled to injunctive relief, specific performance restoring the original operating state, and full monetary damages for lost usufruct.

Now look at the fourth panel: Model Three, the foreclosure of maintenance.

Here, an auxiliary component inevitably reaches the end of its natural service life. The main chassis remains sound. The owner or an independent technician can easily replace the worn part with a standard compatible component without compromising core safety. Yet the manufacturer conceals diagnostic protocols, deploys cryptographic serialization locks that reject unbranded parts, or blocks calibration software.

Our fifth evidence exposes these practices as void restraints, artificial hoarding, and the exploitation of distress.

When title passes, the right to maintain that chattel passes with it. Any clause or digital lock that forbids an owner from maintaining their own property strikes at the very essence of the contract (*muqtadâ al-aqd*). Under Islamic law, any contractual term that strips the buyer of essential ownership rights while granting the maker an ongoing commercial monopoly is a void condition (*şart-ı fâsid*), null and void from the outset. Furthermore, when a maker ties component replacement to exclusive master keys, they fabricate artificial scarcity. Modern computer architecture refutes the maker's false dilemma: zero-knowledge proofs and compartmentalized attestation protocols allow hardware validation without exposing proprietary secrets. Hiding behind safety to monopolize repair is an actionable abuse of rights. Imam Abu Yusuf defined *ihtikâr* as the deliberate withholding of any commodity or service whose denial inflicts severe hardship upon the public; contemporary jurists apply this principle directly to diagnostic tools and replacement parts. By systematically closing all repair avenues, the maker forces the consumer into an impossible bind: pay extortionate monopoly repair rates or throw the machine into the trash. The Prophet, peace be upon him, explicitly prohibited *bay'u'l-mudtarr*—exploiting the duress and distress of another to extract an unconscionable bargain. Under Article 100 of the Mecelle, all such monopolistic locks are legally void, and the owner's right to independent repair is absolute.

Look now at the fifth panel, Model Four, which charts systemic sector collapse and the injury to the commons.

When these predatory practices spread across an entire market, individual misconduct hardens into an oligopolistic cartel. Bound by high switching costs, consumers face a rigged game where escape is impossible.

Our sixth evidence speaks to systemic waste and the inviolability of the planetary commons—*Hukukullâh*.

Premature obsolescence imposes an unbearable ecological toll (*ifsâd fi'l-arz*). In life-cycle assessment, the dominant environmental cost of durable goods is not merely the disposal volume, but the vast thermodynamic, mineral, and chemical debt incurred during initial extraction and fabrication. When a complex asset is discarded prematurely, the initial ecological investment has not been amortized, and the maker forces the biosphere to absorb unnecessary entropy. Manufacturers cannot hide behind fractional energy efficiency gains in newer models: life-cycle accounting proves that marginal operational improvements almost never offset the embedded ecological footprint of manufacturing a replacement from scratch. Article 26 of the Mecelle dictates that private commercial convenience must be curtailed to avert universal harm (*zarar-ı âmm*). Contractual freedom (*el-hurriyyetü't-teâkudiyye*) is subordinate to the public interest (*el-maslahatü'l-âmme*). Private parties cannot contract away the thermodynamic carrying capacity of the earth. The public boundary (*Hukukullâh*) is non-negotiable.

Our seventh evidence highlights the legislative wisdom behind this prohibition: the destruction of mutual trust.

In the science of legal methodology, social friction and enmity (*adâvet*) are not the technical operative cause (*illet*) of a prohibition; the operative cause must be objective and measurable—here, latent defect, unconscionable disparity, and unlawful destruction. Enmity is the overarching legislative wisdom (*hikmet*). As Surah Al-Ma'idah declares in verse 91: "Satan only seeks to sow enmity and hatred among you." When an asset fails through natural wear, the owner accepts the reality of physical entropy without bitterness. But the moment a consumer discovers an artificial software throttle or a deliberate repair lockout, the commercial relationship transforms into an experience of captive exploitation. The resulting public fury fuels class-action lawsuits, consumer cynicism, and a systemic breakdown of trust in commerce.

Our eighth evidence exposes the destruction of honest commerce through adverse selection.

When durability is concealed, honest manufacturers who invest in long-lasting products are penalized. Because buyers cannot easily distinguish genuine durability from superficial marketing, they refuse to pay the necessary premium for quality. Deceptive producers who cut manufacturing costs and harvest repeated replacement sales expand their market share, steadily driving conscientious manufacturers into insolvency. In classical economics, inferior goods lead to falling prices. But in captive platform regimes, switching costs prevent this correction. Consumers remain trapped, paying premium prices for disposable goods. This is corrupt industry practice (*örf-i fâsid*). Under Islamic jurisprudence, custom that violates statutory norms or public interest carries zero legal authority, even if adopted by an entire industry. The public regulatory authority (*hisbe*) is under a mandatory duty to intervene, nullify collusive practices, and establish compulsory durability standards to protect the public trust.

Now let us confront the four primary defenses offered by manufacturers.

First, the defense of contract: "The buyer accepted our terms of service and software license. There was full disclosure and consent."

This defense fails on two distinct levels. In private contract law, consent must be free, genuine, and directed toward a lawful subject matter. Manufacturers never openly disclose their premature termination routines; their business model depends upon charging for sound life while secretly delivering a truncated good. Furthermore, terms that grant a seller the ongoing power to destroy conveyed property are void ab initio. A standard adhesion contract cannot sanitize an unlawful act. More fundamentally, even if a maker stamped in bold letters on the box, "This machine will self-destruct after four units of time and cannot be repaired," that disclosure only addresses bilateral deception; it does not authorize the destruction of our shared planetary commons. Private individuals cannot lawfully consent to *ifsâd fi'l-arz*. The regulatory authority is duty-bound to prohibit such waste regardless of private agreement.

Second, the defense of necessity: "We had no alternative. We intervened to protect system stability, battery safety, and regulatory compliance."

This argument seeks to convert technical necessity (*TeknikZaruret*) into an absolute shield. But the law rejects false dilemmas. Necessity is strictly bounded by proportionality under Article 22 of the Mecelle. When a minor internal battery exhausts, that localized wear does not give the manufacturer license to throttle the entire system and force the customer into buying a new machine. More decisively, Article 33 of the Mecelle establishes an unbending rule: *Al-idtirâru lâ yubtilu haqqa'l-gayr*—necessity does not extinguish the vested property right of another. If an authentic hazard arises, the maker must issue transparent notices, provide replacement components at reasonable cost, and restore the device to sound operation. Packaging commercial obsolescence as safety is not engineering; it is corporate subterfuge masquerading as necessity.

Turn now to the sixth panel on your screen, which resolves the third defense: intellectual property.

Manufacturers argue: "We sold the physical chassis, but we retained the copyright in the governing code. Modern connected devices are continuous dynamic services, not static personal property."

This defense seeks to exploit intellectual property to dissolve private personal ownership. The solution does not deny intellectual property; rather, it applies the universal First Sale Doctrine—known in Islamic jurisprudence as patent and copyright exhaustion (*İstihlâk-ı Hak bi'l-Bey'i'l-Evvel*). The moment a manufacturer sells a good for a full purchase price *Semen*, its legal power to control the physical use, operation, and maintenance of that specific, individual copy (*Milk al-Nuskha*) is completely exhausted. The maker retains the exclusive right to prevent third parties from pirating and commercializing the code base; but they acquire zero authority to remotely burn the conveyed machine. To invoke copyright as a pretext for remote throttling or repair foreclosure is an abuse of right and a void condition (*şart-ı fâsid*) under Article 100 of the Mecelle. Ongoing connected update channels are held in trust (*amânat al-hifz*). The manufacturer may deploy defensive patches; they cannot weaponize that trust to inflict property damage. The legal duty is clear: makers must remove proprietary serialization locks that block compatible parts, and when cloud services are retired, they must release diagnostic keys to restore standalone local autonomy to the owner. If a company wants continuous governance, let them operate a genuine lease (*icâre*) where the asset stays on their balance sheet and they bear all repair costs. You cannot collect the purchase price of an outright sale while acting as a perpetual feudal landlord.

Fourth, the defense of innovation: "Planned obsolescence funds research and development. Rapid replacement cycles drive technological progress and lower unit costs."

This argument collapses under contractual, ethical, and economic scrutiny. First, look at the moral logic: the manufacturer secretly deprives the buyer of agreed lifespan to fund future prototypes. The value of that undelivered utility is taken without consent. A noble end cannot sanctify an unlawful means (*al-ghâyatu lâ tubarriru'l-wasîlah*). If buyers truly wanted to fund future research through premature product death, makers would disclose it openly; their secrecy is an admission that consumers would reject the deal. Second, real technological progress thrives on the voluntary adoption of superior performance, not the forced destruction of existing utility. When older hardware cannot support demanding modern software, the maker is entitled to stabilize the legacy operating system and announce the end of major feature upgrades. Preserving a stable baseline maintains functional integrity without destroying the machine. Genuine innovation elevates the production frontier; planned obsolescence destroys existing wealth to generate unearned repeat sales.

Look now at the final panel: our synoptic matrix and conclusive rulings.

The legal, rational, and moral verdict on planned obsolescence is absolute wherever an artificial limit or ecosystem lock-in is imposed without strict proportionality:

First: Embedding artificial failure ceilings into durable goods or blocking independent maintenance is upstream commercial deception (*taghrîr*), latent defect (*ayb*), and actionable supply-chain fraud.

Second: Remotely degrading a device post-conveyance or using digital locks to obstruct repair is an unlawful tortious trespass (*mübâşereten teaddî*), a betrayal of custodial trust, and a violation of title.

Third: When cumulative obsolescence breaches the carrying capacity of the commons, it generates irrecoverable planetary injury (*ifsâd fi'l-arz* and *darar-ı âmm*). This invalidates all contractual exculpatory clauses without diminishing the owner's title, triggering direct regulatory sanctions and restitution against the manufacturer.

Fourth: Dressing these practices in the corporate vocabulary of license, safety, ecosystem integrity, or innovation does not diminish the wrong; it merely compounds public injury with legal evasion.

For manufacturers and platform architects, the boundary of justice is unmistakable:

If your device reaches its computational or physical limits, declare that milestone transparently, leave the software stable, and decouple authentication keys to preserve local autonomy.

If an authentic safety hazard or localized fatigue arises, issue an open notice, enable modular replacement under the proportionality rule of Article 22, and never disable the machine.

If system integrity requires validation, deploy open, compartmentalized protocols rather than proprietary monopoly locks that outlaw independent repair.

Transactions must be honest in form and reality. If you sell an asset, you must relinquish proprietary control. If you wish to retain ongoing governance, you must adopt a genuine circular lease (*icâre*) and bear the entire operational and life-cycle risk on your own balance sheet.

Any corporate scheme that collects the upfront price of an absolute sale while secretly retaining the remote power to extinguish the good is not commerce. It is an unlawful commercial tyranny.

Thank you.
