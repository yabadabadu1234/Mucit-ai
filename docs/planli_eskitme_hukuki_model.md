Today I will speak about planned obsolescence and its legal, rational and moral ruling.
I will not argue from objects. A machine, a part, a battery or a program carries its own physics, its own safety rules and its own laws, and the moment I name one of them, the argument turns into a debate about that thing and I lose the principle. So I will take the principle out of every object. In the language of the jurists this is the purification of the ground, tenkîh-i menât: I remove the brand, the model and the material, and I keep only what the ruling really depends on. What remains is a model with economic architecture, cyber-physical ecosystems, strict delictual liability, and objective normative variables. Every example in this talk is built only on this model and on its four forms.

Here are the variables. 
The maker or platform architect is M (al-Sâni'). The retail vendor or intermediate seller who transfers custody is S (al-Bâi'). The end-user buyer or owner is B (al-Mushtarî). 
The durable cyber-physical good is X. The purchase price paid by B is P. 
The benchmark baseline durability of X—defined by reference state-of-the-art engineering standards, material endurance under normal usage profiles, and uncorrupted baseline design potential—is E, measured in units of operational time. 
The premature operational termination, degradation, or artificial lockout engineered by M is L, also measured in units of operational time, where L is strictly shorter than E.
The technical filter is T: governed by proportionality (al-tenâsüb) and prohibition of the abuse of rights (sû-i isti'mâl-i hak, Mecelle Article 22), where a marginal engineering pretext cannot justify the severe destruction of utility (E - L).
The platform switching cost and ecosystem dependency is W (Walled Garden / Lock-in): representing the cumulative barrier—proprietary data formats, tied peripheral hardware, non-exportable cloud archives, and software investments—that binds B to M's platform. Where W is high, B is deprived of effective market substitution and forced into captive repeat transactions.
And the public ecological and economic commons threshold is C: the non-negotiable boundary of public interest (maslahah 'âmmah / darar âmm) that invalidates private contractual destruction of finite planetary resources regardless of individual buyer consent.

THE ROOT CHARACTERIZATION: THE FIVE JURISTIC HARMONIES.
Before examining the models, the legal and economic reality of planned obsolescence must be freed from five critical juristic misconceptions:

First, The Boundaries of Negative Restraint versus Positive Servitude (Menfî Def'-i Teaddî ile Müsbet Angarya Ayrımı):
The law does not impose upon private manufacturers an affirmative servitude or public-utility forced labor to engineer free APIs, indefinite support, or perpetual diagnostic infrastructure. The command of tort and delict is strictly negative: to refrain from affirmative harm, active sabotage, and monopolistic closure (Kef'-i yed ve men'-i teaddî). When M sells X, M is not required to maintain X forever; M is forbidden from deploying affirmative software traps, serialization kill-switches, and aggressive legal/cryptographic barriers that actively paralyze independent repair. The demand of justice is not positive servitude, but negative cessation of interference: cease active obstruction, remove artificial tethers, and let the lawful owner maintain his property.

Second, Pure Economic Loss versus Upstream Fraud and Post-Sale Trespass (Ayb ile İtlâf ve Economic Loss Doktrini Ayrımı):
In tort law, the economic loss doctrine bars delictual recovery when a product merely disappoints commercial expectations or deteriorates through internal defect without harming persons or separate property. The model strictly honors this boundary:
In Model One (Factory Phase), premature termination is legally characterized not as a violent external trespass (itlâf), but as an upstream commutative deceit (al-taghrîr al-fi'lî) and intentional delivery of a defective benchmark asset, enforceable through contractual defect options (hıyâru'l-ayb, Mecelle Article 337) against vendor S, with statutory indemnification cascading up the commercial chain to M, and direct regulatory sanctions by the hisbah.
In Models Two and Three (Post-Sale Phase), M's liability is not barred by the economic loss doctrine because M commits an affirmative, post-conveyance external trespass (mubâşereten teaddî ve itlâf) upon already-vested external property across communication networks, and imposes an artificial monopolistic restraint upon external chattel.

Third, The Risk of Time versus Sabotage: Honoring Al-Kharâju bi'd-Damân (Zaman Riski ile Kasıtlı İtlaf Ayrımı):
Under the foundational Hadith and maxim codified in Mecelle Article 87 ("Al-kharâju bi'd-damân" / Revenue follows liability for loss) and Article 85 ("Al-gurmu bi'l-gunm" / Burden follows benefit), the buyer who reaps the operational yield of a durable asset after delivery (kabz) justly bears the natural temporal risks of physical wear, chemical entropy, and genuine technological depreciation (amortisman ve meşru kesâd). The manufacturer is never an open-ended insurer against time or entropy. The model does not shift post-delivery risk onto M: natural fatigue is strictly borne by B. What the law prohibits is not the natural wear borne by the owner, but M's affirmative, unannounced planting of artificial self-destruct mechanisms and post-sale remote degradation. B assumes the risk of nature; B never assumes the risk of hidden sabotage by the maker.

Fourth, Platform Captivity versus Atomistic Market Illusion (Ekosistem Rehineliği ve Ağ Tahakkümü):
Planned obsolescence cannot survive in an atomistic spot market with perfect substitution; an unencumbered buyer whose toaster breaks simply buys a competitor's brand. Planned obsolescence thrives exclusively within platform monopolies and high switching-cost environments (W), where M holds B's personal data, software licenses, and accessory ecosystem hostage. Captive repeat demand is not consumer loyalty; it is manufactured exploitation of distress (ihtikâr ve bay' al-mudtarr), where B is coerced into repurchasing from M to avoid the catastrophic capital loss of abandoning W.

Fifth, The Primacy of the Commons over Private Consent (Rıza ile Kamu Maslahatının Hiyerarşisi):
Private contractual autonomy (al-hurriyyah al-ta'âqudiyyah) governs purely private rights (hakk-ı sırf); it carries zero authority to legitimize systemic ecological degradation, toxic e-waste accumulation, and resource squandering (ifsâd fi'l-arz / hakk-ı âm). Even if M places a prominent disclosure on the packaging and B explicitly consents to purchase an asset engineered to brick in three years, that disclosure merely remedies private deceit (taghrîr); it leaves the systemic public offense (darar âmm) fully intact. The public supervisory authority (hisbah ve siyâsah shar'iyyah) possesses the peremptory duty to ban engineered obsolescence ex officio, because private consent can never legalize public harm or the destruction of the commons.

Planned obsolescence, as an actionable commercial wrong and systemic delict, exists wherever an artificial limit L or ecosystem restraint is imposed without strictly proportionate technical necessity, truncating baseline utility and externalizing ecological damage onto society.
The procedural rule of adjudication is objective: B or the regulatory authority presents a prima facie showing of premature failure, statistical fleet anomaly, or artificial lockout. Evidentiary tools—neutral expert (ahl al-khibrah) teardowns, reverse engineering, and telemetry audits—shift the burden to M to demonstrate that no less restrictive, open alternative could preserve functionality.

By planned obsolescence I mean an affirmative design choice, architectural lockout, or post-sale intervention by the maker that artificially and disproportionately shortens the useful life of a good, or affirmatively extinguishes the owner's legal ability to maintain and operate it. Three boundaries delineate legitimate commerce from unlawful destruction:
First, natural material wear, chemical aging, and entropy, which are borne by the owner under al-kharâju bi'd-damân.
Second, authentic computational exhaustion: where older hardware lacks the physical computational capacity to run newly developed programs, M may cease feature upgrades, leaving legacy software intact in its working state.
Third, third-party cloud service conclusion: where an external cloud service terminates with reasonable notice, provided M does not affirmatively brick the local hardware or deploy cryptographic locks to block independent operation.
Crucially, declaring a premature design limit openly on the packaging does not sanitize the act: disclosure resolves bilateral deceit, but leaves the systemic ecological offense against the commons subject to regulatory invalidation and prohibition.

The talk has two sides. On the first side stand our evidences, eight of them, built on four models. On the second side stand the producer defenses, four of them, and I will refute each one.

MODEL ONE: THE LIMIT PLANTED BEFORE THE SALE.
Here is the model. M manufactures cyber-physical asset X. In manufacturing, M has two design configurations, A one and A two. A one grants X its customary durable lifespan E. A two imposes an artificial design limit, glue-sealed obsolescence, or underspecified weak component that terminates X at L. Between A one and A two, any claimed technical or cost difference T is either nonexistent or grossly trivial and disproportionate to the massive loss of operational life (E - L). B is bound to M's platform by switching costs W. M chooses A two to accelerate replacement demand within its captive ecosystem. At time t zero, B observes customary market longevity E and pays price P. For clarity of demonstration, let E be ten units of operational time and L be four units of operational time. P was determined upon the expectation of ten units.

OUR FIRST EVIDENCE: COMMUTATIVE DISPARITY, LATENT DEFECT, AND UPSTREAM FRAUD.
The claim: in commutative transactions, baseline value is determined by operational durability; introducing an artificial structural limitation constitutes an actionable upstream defect (ayb) and fraudulent disparity (gabn-i fâhiş bi't-taghrîr), enforceable via contractual rescission against S and upstream restitutionary indemnification against M.
The proof: durable goods are acquired for their stream of utility over time. When M manufactures asset X with engineered limit L, M introduces an objective defect into the commercial supply chain. B and intermediate vendor S evaluate the asset based on standard durability E. While contract law tolerates minor subjective variance (gabn-i yesîr), neutral appraisers (ahl al-khibrah) evaluate the true financial value of an asset whose operational life is structurally curtailed from ten units to four. Because an asset designed to fail prematurely loses a major proportion of its commercial valuation at the very moment of exchange, the resulting gap between the price paid P and the true objective value constitutes an unconscionable disparity (gabn-i fâhiş) accompanied by active fraudulent concealment (taghrîr). Under the economic loss doctrine and classical sales jurisprudence, this upstream factory-phase flaw is strictly categorized as an original latent defect (ayb-i kadîm / ayb-i hafî)—not an external physical trespass (itlâf)—because the failure occurs purely within the internal commercial boundary of the good itself.
The Qur'an commands in Surah An-Nisa, verse 29: "Do not consume your wealth among yourselves by false ways." Extracting the full market price of a durable good while delivering an asset burdened with engineered mortality is consuming property by falsehood (ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl).
The rule: against immediate seller S, under Mecelle Article 356, B possesses the contractual remedy of rescission (faskh): B may cancel the contract and recover purchase price P upon tendering restitution. Under Mecelle Article 358, where restitution of the physical good is barred due to alteration or consumption, contractual rescission strictly lapses. Crucially, intermediate seller S is an innocent retailer; once S compensates B or accepts rescission, S possesses the established right of upstream recourse (rücû') against manufacturer M to recover all disbursed sums. Furthermore, where M organized the deceptive distribution scheme, public commercial law and hisbah regulations compel M to directly disgorge the unearned price differential (noksan-ı semen).

OUR SECOND EVIDENCE: OBJECTIVE LATENT DEFECT AND CASCADE INDEMNIFICATION.
The claim: an engineered durability limit L constitutes an objective original latent defect (ayb-i hafî), entitling B to contractual defect remedies (khiyâr al-ayb) against S and cascade indemnification from M, without requiring proof of subjective internal malice.
The proof: durability in a durable cyber-physical asset is an implied core condition of fitness and merchantable quality. M alone holds the proprietary blueprints, telemetry, and source code containing limit L; neither intermediate seller S nor buyer B has the means of discovering it at t zero. The distribution of an artificially curtailed good into commerce creates an objective misrepresentation by conduct, warranting that the good conforms to sound custom. The Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade the transaction infected with gharar, reported by Muslim. He also stated: "Whoever deceives us is not one of us," reproaching the merchant who concealed wet grain beneath dry grain. Hidden limit L is the wet grain at the bottom of the vessel. Liability for delivering an inherently defective asset does not depend upon proving corporate malice; it is an objective breach of the implied warranty of merchantability.
The instance: B examines X at purchase and finds no visible flaw; the premature termination code, underspecified capacitor, or adhesive barrier resides in M's proprietary design.
The rule: under the governing law of latent defects (khiyâr al-ayb, Mecelle Article 337), B holds the fundamental option to return X to S and recover the full price P. Crucially, where restitution is rendered legally or physically impossible—such as where prolonged usage constitutes a legal bar to return (mâni'-i radd)—the action does not perish. Under Mecelle Articles 343, 344, and 345, B is entitled to sue directly for the pecuniary price diminution (noksan-ı semen / arsh). Under the rules of commercial subrogation, retail vendor S cascades this liability directly to manufacturer M. This preserves both the privity of contract and the economic loss rule: contractual ayb governs the internal failure of the good, while tortious itlâf is reserved for post-sale external trespasses.

OUR THIRD EVIDENCE: MANUFACTURED CAPTIVITY, UNLAWFUL ENRICHMENT, AND THE PREEMPTION OF BENEFIT.
The claim: no commercial entity may manufacture platform captivity to profit from the engineered destruction of its own products.
The proof: in a competitive, unencumbered market, an early failure would drive B to a competitor. But M constructs high switching costs W—locking B's data, peripheral accessories, and software ecosystem within M's walled garden. When M curtails X to L, M does not face competitive elimination; instead, M exploits B's captive state to force a repeat purchase. M's accelerated revenue is derived directly from B's artificial deprivation. A nominal engineering pretext T cannot sanitize this destruction: where the alleged technical saving is trivial while the loss of life is severe, the defense of necessity is defeated by the doctrine of proportionality (Mecelle Article 22). 
The legal principles are two. First, Mecelle Article 19: "Harm is eliminated" (Al-dararu yuzâl). Second, the fundamental maxim codified in Mecelle Article 99: "Men iste'cele şey'en kable evânihî ûkıbe bi-hırmânih" (Whoever hastens something before its due time by an illegitimate means is penalized with deprivation thereof). In strict liability, whether categorized as direct destruction (mubâşir, Mecelle Article 92) or causal inducement (mutasabbib, Mecelle Article 93), the objective reality is that M engineered the premature failure to harvest repeat revenue; the law deprives M of that ill-gotten benefit through mandatory restitution, restitutionary damages (arsh), and the disgorgement of unearned replacement profits.
The instance: at four units of time, X fails prematurely. Because B's accumulated digital assets are locked in M's platform (W), B is economically coerced to repurchase from M, accelerating M's revenue cycle at the direct expense of B's wealth.

MODEL TWO: THE ACT ON THE GOOD AFTER THE SALE.
The model: the transaction is finalized at t zero, and complete ownership and physical custody reside with B. No imminent peril to life or cryptographic breakdown arises, or any claimed justification T is grossly disproportionate to the damage inflicted. X performs every warranted function. At a later time t one, M utilizes a retained remote telemetry channel or silent firmware push to unilaterally throttle performance, disable existing features, or trigger hardware rejection of authentic replacement parts.

OUR FOURTH EVIDENCE: UNLAWFUL TRESPASS, DIRECT DESTRUCTION, AND ABUSE OF CUSTODIAL ACCESS.
The claim: upon conveyance of ownership, M's proprietary authority over X is completely extinguished; any subsequent unilateral adverse modification constitutes an unlawful trespass and direct property destruction (mubâşereten itlâf).
The proof: sale transfers exclusive ownership (milk tamm) to B, encompassing the sole right to use, preserve, alter, or repair the cyber-physical asset. When M argues that B consented to background updates via an electronic adhesion contract, contract law dictates that consent given for operational security and maintenance cannot be converted into a license to impair the good. In Islamic jurisprudence, custodial authority delegated for maintenance and preservation (amânat al-hifz) strictly excludes any act of impairment (itlâf); exceeding delegated custodial boundaries constitutes unauthorized disposal in another's property (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr) and an actionable breach of trust. Under Mecelle Article 92, the direct actor (mubâşir) is strictly liable for property damage (itlâf) regardless of whether the act was executed with subjective malice or claimed administrative convenience.
The instance: at t one, X is functioning properly. M pushes a remote firmware change. B discovers that performance is throttled or third-party replacement batteries are blocked. M has directly invaded and impaired B's property.
The rule: M executed the code modification directly across the network, acting as the direct tortfeasor (mubâshir). Under Mecelle Article 92, M is strictly liable for property damage. B is entitled to injunctive relief, specific performance—the full restoration of X to its warranted operating state—and compensatory damages for the incurred devaluation and lost utility.

MODEL THREE: THE CLOSING OF REPAIR.
The model: X suffers an ordinary mechanical or electronic wear fault. B, or an independent qualified technician, can repair it. The repair involves standard replacement parts and does not compromise biometric root keys or statutory emission safety, so any technical excuse T is either nonexistent or entirely disproportionate. M withholds diagnostic error codes, pairs parts through proprietary cryptographic serialization, or shuts down independent service tools.

OUR FIFTH EVIDENCE: VOID RESTRAINTS, MANUFACTURED DISTRESS, AND MONOPOLISTIC IMPEDIMENTS.
The claim: when M conditions the operation of X upon an absolute prohibition of external repair, M imposes a void restraint that contradicts the essence of ownership, manufactures artificial scarcity, and exploits the owner's necessitous state.
The proof has three steps. 
First, an absolute conveyance inherently transfers the unrestricted right of physical maintenance to the owner. A restraint imposed by M—whether by contractual clause, cryptographic parts-pairing, or software locks—prohibiting the owner from opening, repairing, or servicing his property directly contradicts the essence of sale (muqtadâ al-aqd). Under Islamic jurisprudence, a condition that strips the buyer of the essential prerogatives of ownership while securing an unearned monopoly for the maker is a corrupting condition (shart fâsid), rendering the restriction null and void ab initio. 
Second, when M monopolizes diagnostic tools and serialization keys under the pretext of security, M manufactures artificial scarcity (ihtikâr) in essential maintenance. While collective network security is a legitimate concern, genuine security is achieved through open, interoperable cryptographic standards, not corporate monopoly. Imam Abu Yusuf broadened the doctrine of ihtikâr to encompass any commodity or service whose deliberate withholding inflicts public hardship. Contemporary jurists, reasoning by systematic extrapolation (takhrîj), extend this prohibition to essential repair parts, diagnostics, and serialization pairing whose monopolistic withholding inflicts systemic hardship (haraj) on the public. 
Third, by locking all independent repair doors, M forces the owner into a state of captive distress (iztirâr), compelling B to pay whatever extortionate price M dictates or prematurely discard X. Abu Dawud reports from Ali, may God be pleased with him, that the Prophet forbade the exploitative transaction forced upon a person in distress (bay' al-mudtarr).
The instance: an unbonded physical screen or sensor fails. M's serialization lock rejects an identical, certified replacement, forcing B to pay an extortionate fee at M's exclusive facility or discard X.
The rule: under Mecelle Article 100, M's attempt to restrict completed ownership is legally void. The restrictive lock is an unlawful impediment, and B has the untrammeled right to repair his property through any competent provider.

MODEL FOUR: THE PLATFORM AND SECTOR DYNAMICS.
The model: Models One, Two and Three expand from isolated practices into an industry-wide platform oligopoly or cartel. In this market, manufacturers leverage high platform switching costs W (data lock-in, proprietary accessories, closed cloud APIs) to extinguish consumer exit. The model analyzes two successive phases: the informational opacity phase where captive repeat purchases occur, followed by the revelation phase where collective waste, resource squandering, and public harm erupt.

OUR SIXTH EVIDENCE: SYSTEMIC SQUANDERING AND THE INALIENABLE COMMONS.
The claim: premature artificial obsolescence externalizes catastrophic ecological burdens onto the planetary commons (ifsâd fi'l-arz), a public harm (darar âmm) that cannot be validated or legalized by private buyer consent.
The proof: in a linear or partially circular industrial cycle, truncating the operational lifespan of a good from E to L forces society to extract, transport, manufacture, and discard materials at an accelerated velocity. Even accounting for modern recycling, material recovery suffers massive energetic, thermodynamic, and toxic losses. The United Nations Global E-waste Monitor documented sixty-two million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022, less than a quarter formally recycled, identifying shortened life cycles and restricted repairability as primary drivers. 
Regarding causal liability: B is merely the innocent consumer trapped within M's locked ecosystem. M is the platform architect who engineered premature mortality. In tort, M's liability is objective: M introduces an engineered agent of destruction into the public stream of commerce.
The foundational maxims of the Mecelle govern this destruction: a private commercial interest is overridden to repel a general public harm (Article 26); the greater harm is removed by the lesser (Article 27); and the burden follows the benefit (Mecelle Article 85: "Al-gurmu bi'l-gunm"). In Islamic jurisprudence, private contractual autonomy (al-hurriyyah al-ta'âqudiyyah) is strictly bounded by public welfare (al-maslahah al-'âmmah). Just as two private parties cannot contract to poison a public river, an informed buyer and a manufacturer cannot contract to squander scarce resources and poison the biosphere. The ecological commons (hakk-ı âm) is inalienable.
The instance: a population of N users requires durable utility over a timeline of twelve units. When E equals ten, replacement demand is minimal; when L is curtailed to four within locked platforms, the volume of extracted, processed, and discarded units multiplies, needlessly poisoning the earth.

OUR SEVENTH EVIDENCE: LEGISLATIVE WISDOM: ENMITY AND THE CORROSION OF TRUST.
The claim: the systemic practice of planned obsolescence breeds widespread social hostility and institutional cynicism once discovered, defeating the overarching legislative purpose of commercial ethics.
In juristic methodology, enmity is not the operative legal ground (illa) of the prohibition, because an illa must be an objective, measurable attribute. The operative grounds are objective constructive destruction (helâk-ı hükmî), unconscionable disparity, and tortious damage (itlâf). Enmity is the underlying legislative wisdom (hikma) of the prohibition. The Qur'an states in Surah Al-Ma'idah, verse 91: "Satan only seeks to sow enmity and hatred among you." The identical wisdom underpins the prohibition of commercial betrayal and platform entrapment.
The proof: when a cyber-physical device perishes of natural wear or unpreventable economic obsolescence, B harbors no grievance; he recognizes physical reality. But when investigative disclosure, source code audits, or whistleblowing reveals that the failure was programmed by design or enforced by serialization locks, consumer perception transforms from ordinary trade into captive exploitation. The resulting friction erupts into collective litigation, consumer boycotts, and systemic market distrust.
The instance: independent security researchers expose the hidden limit L or the artificial throttling patch. The revelation destroys goodwill, replacing market trust with defensive hostility.
The rule: the prohibition stands firmly upon its operative grounds of latent defect, constructive destruction, and delictual damage, and the eruption of public enmity confirms the legislative wisdom of the law.

OUR EIGHTH EVIDENCE: ADVERSE SELECTION AND THE CAPTIVITY OF HONEST TRADE.
The claim: during the period of information opacity—before consumers uncover the artificial limit—dishonest goods systematically drive durable, honest goods out of the marketplace.
The proof: consumers cannot directly verify the internal lifespan or uncorrupted design of a complex cyber-physical good at the point of retail sale. Prior to widespread exposure, market prices are formed based on observable external features. Because buyers cannot detect the hidden difference between E and L, an honest producer M1 who builds X for durability E cannot command a fair price premium. Meanwhile, producer M2 who limits X to L accelerates his repurchase cycle within locked ecosystem W; uninformed buyers, attributing early failure to bad luck or battery fatigue, buy again from M2 because switching to M1 entails abandoning their accumulated data and accessories. M2 captures outsized replacement revenues and aggressively expands marketing, gradually bankrupting M1 until only truncated goods survive on the market.
The rule: harm must be eliminated (Mecelle Article 19), and private gain cannot be tolerated when it destroys market integrity (Mecelle Article 26). Here the procedural duality of Islamic law resolves the tension between micro-contract remedies and macro-market order: while individual buyers utilize contractual options (hıyârât) against retailers S and product liability claims against makers M, prolonged societal use and widespread adoption can never convert a predatory practice into binding law. Under the foundational maxims of Islamic jurisprudence, a corrupt custom (al-'urf al-fâsid) that conflicts with clear legal texts and general welfare carries zero normative authority—even if it achieves universal industry prevalence. Consequently, the public regulatory authority (hisbah and siyâsah shar'iyyah) bears the non-delegable duty to intervene ex officio: invalidating collusive industry conventions, enforcing statutory transparency and minimum durability benchmarks, and penalizing engineered obsolescence to preserve authentic commerce and public trust.

THE JURISTIC ACCOUNTING OF GROUNDS AND SYSTEMIC HARMS.
To preserve precise methodology, the grounds are categorized into operative legal grounds (illal) and objective rational harms (mafâsid):

Six operative legal grounds (illal) derive from the texts, contract law, and the law of delict:
1. Eating wealth unjustly (ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl), in Evidence 1.
2. Gross disparity between price and utility (gabn-i fâhiş), in Evidence 1.
3. Concealment and unannounced defects (taghrîr and ayb-i hafî), in Evidence 2.
4. Objective constructive destruction and tortious damage (helâk-ı hükmî ve itlâf, Mecelle Articles 92 and 93), in Evidences 1, 3 and 4.
5. Unauthorized disposal and abuse of custodial access (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr ve teaddî), in Evidence 4.
6. The corrupting restraint, artificial withholding, and exploitation of captive distress (shart fâsid, ihtikâr, and bay' al-mudtarr), in Evidences 3 and 5.

Four objective rational and systemic harms (mafâsid) derive from economic facts and legal maxims:
7. Unlawful preemption and premature appropriation of benefit (isticel, Mecelle Article 99), in Evidence 3.
8. Systemic misallocation of productive capital, in Evidence 3.
9. Ecological waste and public harm to the commons (darar âmm / ifsâd fi'l-arz, Mecelle Article 26), in Evidence 6.
10. Systemic market collapse through adverse selection and platform lock-in, in Evidence 8.

Enmity (adâvet) remains the overarching legislative wisdom (hikma) and is not counted among the operative causes. 
Under the higher objectives of Islamic law (Maqâsid al-Sharî'ah), planned obsolescence violates the protection of property (hifz al-mâl), commutative justice, social peace, and environmental balance.

NOW THE DEFENSES OF THE PRODUCERS.

The first defense: "The buyer accepted the terms of sale and license agreements. Full disclosure and consent were given."
The refutation: this defense fails on two distinct juristic planes—the plane of private contract and the plane of the public commons.
First, in private contract law, consent must be free, informed, and directed toward a lawful subject matter. Modern manufacturers never disclose engineered mortality; their business model relies on concealing L while charging the price of E. Furthermore, adhesion clauses where B supposedly agrees that M may unilaterally degrade performance or brick functions remotely are void ab initio. In Islamic jurisprudence, a condition that negates the core objective of the contract (muqtadâ al-aqd) is a corrupting condition (shart fâsid). An adhesion screen cannot legalize an unlawful tort or property destruction.
Second, and most fundamentally: even if M placed an explicit notice on the packaging stating: "This device is engineered to terminate at four units of time and cannot be repaired," that disclosure merely cures bilateral deceit (taghrîr); it does not legalize the systemic destruction of the commons. Private consent cannot sanctify public corruption (ifsâd fi'l-arz) or the wanton generation of toxic e-waste (darar âmm). The public authority (hisbah) retains the peremptory obligation to outlaw engineered obsolescence regardless of private contractual assent.

The second defense: "We had no choice. For battery stability, system integrity, or environmental compliance, we had to intervene and limit the good."
The refutation: this is the classic attempt to use a nominal technical factor T as an absolute immunity shield. But modern jurisprudence rejects the binary fallacy: T is governed by proportionality (al-tenâsüb) and the prohibition of abuse of rights (sû-i isti'mâl-i hak).
First, under Mecelle Article 22, "Necessities are measured by their true proportion" (Al-darûrâtü tütekkaderu bi-kaderihâ). When an engineering challenge arises—such as chemical battery aging causing voltage spikes, or new emission standards—a manufacturer cannot seize upon that technical reality as an excuse to impose permanent, unannounced performance throttling that coerces the owner into buying a replacement device. 
Second, necessity does not extinguish liability: Mecelle Article 33 establishes the timeless maxim: "Al-idtirâru lâ yubtilu haqqa'l-ghayr" (Necessity does not invalidate the right of another). Where an authentic, unavoidable safety hazard emerges, M is duty-bound to protect safety, but M cannot inflict uncompensated, permanent property devaluation upon B. Under Islamic product liability principles, M remains strictly bound to remedy the underlying defect: M must issue a transparent safety notification, offer the hardware repair or battery replacement at cost or free of charge under recall, and restore X to sound operating condition. Converting an engineering problem into a silent slowdown that manufactures replacement demand is not safety engineering; it is an unlawful evasion (hile-i şer'iyye) and commercial deceit masquerading as necessity.

The third defense: "We sold the physical hardware, but we retained the software. We licensed the code, and modern connected devices are dynamic cyber-physical services, not static physical chattel."
The refutation: this defense correctly recognizes that modern goods are cyber-physical, but reaches a legally perverse conclusion. Software and connectivity must be analyzed across three distinct juristic categories:
First, independent application software acquired separately: this can be legitimately licensed under copyright terms.
Second, embedded firmware and operating systems essential to the hardware's operation: without this code, X is reduced to an inert piece of metal. In jurisprudence, the accessory follows the principal: Mecelle Article 47 dictates that "what is subordinate to a thing in existence is subordinate to it in its legal ruling" (Al-tâbi'u tâbiun), and Article 48 states that "the subordinate cannot be given an independent ruling contrary to the principal." The cyber-physical good is the principal subject of sale; the integrated firmware is its indispensable attribute. The sale of X inherently conveys the permanent right to the functional utility of that embedded code. M retains intellectual property copyrights, but M cannot revoke, throttle, or weaponize the operational capacity of the sold property.
Third, connected network services and ongoing telemetry: connected devices require security patches against evolving threats. Here, ongoing remote access is legally categorized as a delegated custodial trust (amânat al-hifz). M is empowered to deploy defensive security patches; M is strictly forbidden from using this custodial channel to execute adverse property alterations. Furthermore, the law does not demand that M maintain legacy cloud servers forever at its own expense—that would be an unearned positive servitude. Rather, the demand of justice is strictly negative: to cease active obstruction and remove artificial kill-switches. When M legitimately ends cloud support, M must not affirmatively brick the hardware or weaponize cryptographic locks to block independent operation. M must simply step aside: releasing local cryptographic keys and open diagnostic access so the owner or independent technicians can sustain the asset autonomously. If M refuses this separation and insists on perpetual, exclusive unilateral control, M must structure the transaction honestly as an ongoing lease (ijârah) and price it as rent, assuming the continuous upkeep liabilities of an owner-lessor.

The fourth defense: "Planned obsolescence finances innovation. Accelerated replacement cycles generate the revenue required for research and development, lowering hedonic unit costs and driving rapid technological progress."
The refutation: this defense fails on contractual, ethical, and economic grounds.
First, put it into the model. M secretly curtails X to L, withholding this from B, and justifies the premature replacement sale as financing the next generation of goods. B paid for E and received L. The value of the unreceived life was expropriated for an unagreed research fund. A noble end cannot sanctify an unlawful means (al-ghâyatu lâ tubarriru'l-wasîlah). If B were willing to fund future research through premature obsolescence, M would disclose it openly; M's concealment is proof that B would reject the bargain.
Second, hedonic price deflation—the real drop in computing cost per unit (such as Moore's Law)—is an authentic triumph of engineering, but it cannot justify artificial obsolescence. If the next-generation good is genuinely superior, consumers have an authentic incentive to upgrade voluntarily, without their existing property being artificially sabotaged. Hedonic progress thrives on voluntary adoption of better technology, not the forced destruction of existing utility. If older hardware cannot support the demanding computational requirements of newer software, M is fully entitled to freeze feature updates for that hardware, clearly announcing the end of support. Freezing feature enhancements preserves existing functionality without destroying the device. Genuine innovation elevates the new; planned obsolescence murders the old.

CONCLUSION.
The legal, rational, and moral ruling on planned obsolescence is definitively established wherever an artificial limit L or ecosystem lock-in is imposed without proportional necessity, or where a nominal technical pretext T is deployed to engineer unearned replacement sales:
First: to engineer premature failure L into a durable good is an act of upstream commutative deceit (taghrîr), latent defect (ayb), and actionable commercial fraud within the supply chain.
Second: to remotely degrade a good after its sale, or to deploy cryptographic locks that paralyze independent repair, is an unlawful post-sale trespass (mubâşereten teaddî), an abuse of custodial trust, and a nullification of ownership.
Third: to force durable goods prematurely onto waste heaps generates severe, inalienable public harm (ifsâd fi'l-arz / darar âmm) that cannot be validated or sanitized by private contractual consent.
Fourth: to cloak these practices under the labels of license, safety, ecosystem security, or innovation does not diminish the wrong; it merely layers legal evasion upon public destruction.

For manufacturers and platform architects, the boundary is clear and just:
If your hardware reaches its technical limit, declare the end of updates transparently, freeze the software in a stable state, and release local keys and open interfaces to preserve local autonomy.
If an authentic safety hazard arises, intervene openly, issue a recall, and repair the defect without cost to the owner under the doctrine of proportionality.
If collective network security requires cryptographic validation, adopt open, interoperable security standards rather than proprietary monopoly locks.
Either sell the cyber-physical good completely and relinquish your proprietary control, or lease it honestly and shoulder the continuous burdens of maintenance. Any practice that extracts the purchase price of absolute sale while exercising the remote power of guardianship and trapping users in captive obsolescence is not trade; it is an unlawful commercial tyranny. Thank you.
