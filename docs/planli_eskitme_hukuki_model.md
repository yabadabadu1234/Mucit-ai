Today I will speak about planned obsolescence and its legal, rational and moral ruling.
I will not argue from objects. A machine, a part, a battery or a program carries its own physics, its own safety rules and its own laws, and the moment I name one of them, the argument turns into a debate about that thing and I lose the principle. So I will take the principle out of every object. In the language of the jurists this is the purification of the ground, tenkîh-i menât: I remove the brand, the model and the material, and I keep only what the ruling really depends on. What remains is a model with parties, a supply chain, ownership, tort liability and a few rigorous variables. Every example in this talk is built only on this model and on its four forms.

Here are the variables. 
The maker or manufacturer is M (al-Sâni'). The retail vendor or intermediate seller who transfers custody is S (al-Bâi'). The end-user buyer or owner is B (al-Mushtarî). 
The durable cyber-physical good is X. The purchase price paid by B is P. 
The benchmark baseline durability of X—defined by reference state-of-the-art engineering standards, material endurance under normal usage profiles, and uncorrupted baseline design potential—is E, measured in units of operational time. 
The premature operational limit or degradation that M engineers into the design, embeds in firmware, or remotely activates without explicit, informed disclosure is L, also measured in units of operational time, where L is strictly shorter than E.
And there is the technical justification: T. Crucially, T is not a binary switch (0 or 1), because in multi-disciplinary industrial optimization, an engineer can always allege a nominal five-cent material saving, a gram of weight reduction, or a minor chemical safeguard. Rather, T is governed by the universal legal and juristic doctrine of proportionality (al-tenâsüb wa'l-i'tidâl) and the prevention of the abuse of rights (sû-i isti'mâl-i hak). Under Islamic jurisprudence (Mecelle Article 22: "Necessities are estimated according to their true measure"), a marginal, pretextual, or nominal technical benefit T cannot serve as a juristic shield to inflict a grossly disproportionate destruction of utility (E - L) upon the owner, nor can it sanitize an engineered scheme designed to manufacture premature replacement demand.

THE ROOT CHARACTERIZATION: COMMUTATIVE FRAUD, DELICTUAL HARM, AND THE CYBER-PHYSICAL PARADOX.
Before examining the models, the root conflict must be freed from three fatal misconceptions of classical formalisms:

First, the fallacy of privity and the dual nature of liability (Akit ve Haksız Fiil Ayrımı): 
Modern durable goods are rarely purchased directly from the manufacturer M; they flow through intermediate retailers S. A manufacturer cannot hide behind privity of contract (nisbîlik ilkesi) to escape liability. In Islamic jurisprudence and modern commercial law, planned obsolescence is not merely a bilateral sales warranty breach between S and B; it is an actionable product liability and delictual wrong (mas'ûliyyat al-sâni' ve'l-itlâf) committed by M directly against the property of B. When M secretly embeds limit L at the factory, M acts as the intentional causal actor (mutasabbib) who poisons the stream of commerce. When M remotely throttles or bricks X post-sale, M acts as the direct tortfeasor (mubâshir) destroying another's property (itlâf fî milk al-ghayr). While B retains contractual defect remedies (hıyâru'l-ayb) against the immediate seller S, both B and intermediate sellers S hold direct tort and product liability claims against M for fraudulent concealment, unlawful property destruction, and unjust enrichment.

Second, the myth of the binary technical excuse (Tenâsüb ve Ölçülülük İlkesi):
Industrial producers do not confess malice; they cite battery chemistry, water resistance, or cost optimization. But law and equity do not permit a trivial technical tail to wag the dog of ownership. Where the marginal benefit of an undisclosed design choice is negligible (e.g., saving eight cents on a capacitor, or shaving one millimeter off thickness), yet the resulting loss of durability is catastrophic (cutting lifespan from ten units to four), the defense of technical necessity (T) is legally void ab initio. Under Mecelle Article 22 ("Necessities are measured by their true proportion") and the prohibition of legal evasion (hile-i şer'iyye), disproportionate harm unmasks the engineering pretext as an abuse of right.

Third, the cyber-physical ontology (Statik Kütük Dogmasının Aşılması):
A modern durable good is neither an inert wooden chest nor an ephemeral cloud rental; it is a cyber-physical system—a physical chassis animated by embedded logic and connected to a communications grid. Demanding that M sever all digital ties entirely upon sale would leave connected devices vulnerable to zero-day security threats and cryptographic obsolescence, destroying the device by abandonment. Conversely, permitting M to use connectivity as an arbitrary tether converts ownership into a perpetual digital serfdom. 
The juristic resolution is clear: ongoing connection and firmware maintenance are legally categorized as delegated custodial guardianship (amânat al-hifz). Custody authorizes protective fortification; it strictly forbids unilateral impairment, planned degradation, or the closure of independent repair. When M ceases software stewardship, M has no right to brick the asset; M must release local authentication keys and open interfaces, leaving X autonomous in the hands of its owner.

Planned obsolescence, as an unlawful commercial wrong and actionable tort, exists wherever an artificial limit L is imposed without proportional technical necessity, or where a nominal technical pretext T is deployed to engineer unearned replacement sales. 
The procedural rule of adjudication is preserved: B or the regulatory authority presents a prima facie showing of premature failure, statistical fleet anomaly, or artificial lockout. Evidentiary tools—neutral expert (ahl al-khibrah) teardowns, reverse engineering, and telemetry audits—shift the burden to M. To exonerate itself, M cannot merely cite an abstract T; M must prove that the intervention was strictly necessary, strictly proportionate to the objective sought, and incapable of being achieved through less destructive means.

By planned obsolescence I mean a deliberate choice by the maker that artificially and disproportionately shortens the useful life of a good, or extinguishes the owner's legal ability to maintain and repair it. Four things remain strictly outside this definition:
First, natural material wear, which is unintended.
Second, declared design limits or established legitimate custom: where M or S openly discloses the rated operational lifespan (MTBF or rated cycle count) on the packaging or contract, the buyer is bound by explicit contractual stipulation pursuant to the established principle: "Al-Muslimûna 'alâ shurûtihim" (Muslims are bound by their agreed conditions). Where no explicit term is written, standard durability is governed by sound custom (al-'urf al-sahîh) pursuant to Mecelle Article 43: "Al-ma'rûfu 'urfan ka'l-mashrûti shartan" (What is determined by customary practice is like that which is stipulated as an express condition). Crucially, this applies strictly to sound, uncorrupted custom: in Islamic jurisprudence, a corrupt industry practice (al-'urf al-fâsid)—even if collusively adopted across an entire manufacturing sector—can never legitimize systemic deceit (taghrîr), conceal latent flaws (ayb-i hafî), or abrogate normative protective principles.
Third, authentic hardware exhaustion: where older hardware lacks the physical computational capacity to run newly developed programs, M may decline to offer new feature upgrades, leaving legacy software intact in its working state.
Fourth, third-party service discontinuation, provided M did not engineer a concealed, fatal dependency.
M may stop adding features. M may conclude a declared cloud service with reasonable notice and open exit. M may not sabotage, degrade, or hold hostage what has been sold.

The talk has two sides. On the first side stand our evidences, eight of them, built on four models. On the second side stand the producer defenses, four of them, and I will refute each one.

MODEL ONE: THE LIMIT PLANTED BEFORE THE SALE.
Here is the model. M manufactures X. In manufacturing, M has two design configurations, A one and A two. A one grants X its customary durable lifespan E. A two imposes an artificial design limit, glue-sealed obsolescence, or underspecified weak component that terminates X at L. Between A one and A two, any claimed technical or cost difference T is either nonexistent or grossly trivial and disproportionate to the massive loss of operational life (E - L). M chooses A two solely to accelerate replacement demand. At time t zero, B observes customary market longevity E and pays price P, which incorporates that durable utility. For clarity of demonstration, let E be ten units of operational time and L be four units of operational time. P was determined upon the expectation of ten units.

OUR FIRST EVIDENCE: FRAUDULENT DISPARITY, VALUE CONCEALMENT, AND PRODUCT FRAUD.
The claim: in commutative transactions, consent requires transparency regarding the essential attributes that define value; extracting capital through deliberate concealment of an artificial durability defect constitutes unlawful appropriation of property, engaging both contractual rescission against S and direct restitutionary/tort claims against M.
The proof: durable goods are acquired for their stream of utility over time. When M intentionally embeds limit L at production, M manufactures an inherently defective asset, while B and the retail vendor S cannot detect this latent defect at t zero. B pays price P expecting ten units of operational life, but receives an asset engineered to terminate at four. While contract law tolerates minor subjective discrepancies (gabn-i yesîr) in competitive markets, expert appraisers (ahl al-khibrah) evaluate the true financial value of an asset whose life is arbitrarily cut from ten units to four. Because an asset designed to fail prematurely loses a major proportion of its commercial valuation at the very moment of transfer, the resulting gap between the price paid P and the true objective value constitutes an egregious, unconscionable disparity (gabn-i fâhiş) accompanied by active fraudulent concealment (taghrîr).
The Qur'an commands in Surah An-Nisa, verse 29: "Do not consume your wealth among yourselves by false ways." Extracting the full market price of a durable good while concealing an engineered durability flaw that severely depresses its real value is consuming property by falsehood (ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl).
The rule: against the immediate seller S, under Mecelle Article 356, B possesses the contractual remedy of rescission (faskh): B may cancel the contract and recover purchase price P upon tendering restitution of X. In pure classical gabn jurisprudence (Mecelle Article 358), where restitution of the physical good is barred due to alteration, consumption, or destruction, the action for rescission strictly lapses. Crucially, however, privity of contract does not bar recovery against the true perpetrator: against manufacturer M, who engineered the deceitful disparity, B holds a direct, non-contractual claim in tort and commercial fraud (al-taghrîr al-fi'lî ve'l-mas'ûliyyah al-taqsîriyyah), compelling M to disgorge the unjust enrichment and compensate the full diminution of capital.

OUR SECOND EVIDENCE: A CONCEALED ATTRIBUTE, LATENT DEFECT, AND INDEPENDENT PRODUCT LIABILITY.
The claim: when manufacturer M conceals an engineered limit L, the lack of customary durability constitutes an actionable original latent defect (ayb-i hafî), entitling B to contractual defect options (khiyâr al-ayb) against S and direct product liability claims against M.
The proof: durability in a durable cyber-physical asset is an implied core condition of fitness and merchantable quality. M alone holds the proprietary telemetry and hidden blueprint containing limit L; neither intermediate seller S nor buyer B has means of discovering it at t zero. The maker who releases a deliberately crippled good into commerce makes an affirmative misrepresentation by conduct, falsely warranting that the good conforms to sound custom. The Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade the transaction infected with gharar, reported by Muslim. He also stated: "Whoever deceives us is not one of us," reproaching the merchant who concealed wet grain beneath dry grain. Hidden limit L is the wet grain at the bottom of the vessel. In Hanafi jurisprudence, an engineered durability shortfall is an original defect (ayb-i kadîm) embedded at inception.
The instance: B examines X at purchase and finds no visible flaw; the premature termination code or underspecified weak link resides solely in M's proprietary design.
The rule: in contract, under the governing law of latent defects (khiyâr al-ayb, Mecelle Article 337), B holds the fundamental option to return X to S and recover the full price P. Crucially, where restitution is rendered legally or physically impossible—such as where prolonged usage constitutes a legal bar to return (mâni'-i radd)—the action does not perish. Under Mecelle Articles 343, 344, and 345, B is entitled to sue directly for the pecuniary price diminution (noksan-ı semen / arsh). Where seller S is an innocent retailer who compensates B, S is subrogated to B's rights to seek full indemnification from manufacturer M. Furthermore, under universal product liability and the law of delict, B may bypass the retail chain entirely and bring a direct tort claim against M for manufacturing a defective and deceptive product.

OUR THIRD EVIDENCE: DELIBERATE HARM AND UNLAWFUL ACCELERATION OF BENEFIT.
The claim: nobody may intentionally inflict damage upon another's property to derive commercial enrichment from that harm.
The proof: natural degradation is an act of time; engineered failure is an act of design. M engineers failure at four units of operational time specifically so that B must replace X six units earlier than expected. M's accelerated sale is derived directly from B's artificial deprivation. A nominal or pretextual engineering justification T cannot sanctify this destruction: where the alleged technical saving is trivial while the loss of life is severe, the defense of necessity is defeated by the doctrine of proportionality (Mecelle Article 22). Furthermore, the capital that B is forced to spend prematurely is diverted from education, healthcare, and productive welfare into replacing an asset that should not have failed.
The legal principles are two. First, Mecelle Article 19: "Harm is eliminated" (Al-dararu yuzâl). Second, the fundamental maxim codified in Mecelle Article 99: "Men iste'cele şey'en kable evânihî ûkıbe bi-hırmânih" (Whoever hastens something before its due time by an illegitimate means is penalized with deprivation thereof). In classical law, the heir who murders his benefactor to hasten inheritance is disqualified from inheriting. Applying this maxim by strict juristic analogy (qiyâs), M illegitimately hastens the second sale by sabotaging the first; the law deprives M of that ill-gotten benefit. In tort, M is the causal actor (mutasabbib) who acted with deliberate intent, and under Mecelle Article 93, the intentional causer is strictly liable for the resulting damage.
The instance: at four units of time, B is forced to buy a replacement. The replacement was due after ten units, and M has artificially accelerated the second expenditure six units ahead of time.

MODEL TWO: THE ACT ON THE GOOD AFTER THE SALE.
The model: the transaction is finalized at t zero, and complete ownership and physical custody reside with B. No imminent peril to life or cryptographic breakdown arises, or any claimed justification T is grossly disproportionate to the damage inflicted. X performs every warranted function. At a later time t one, M utilizes a retained remote telemetry channel or silent firmware push to unilaterally throttle performance, disable existing features, or trigger hardware rejection of authentic replacement parts.

OUR FOURTH EVIDENCE: UNLAWFUL TRESPASS, DIRECT DESTRUCTION, AND ABUSE OF CUSTODIAL ACCESS.
The claim: upon conveyance of ownership, M's proprietary authority over X is completely extinguished; any subsequent unilateral adverse modification constitutes an unlawful trespass and direct destruction (mubâşereten itlâf) of another's property.
The proof: sale transfers exclusive ownership (milk tamm) to B, encompassing the sole right to use, preserve, alter, or repair the cyber-physical asset. When M argues that B consented to background updates via an electronic adhesion contract, contract law dictates that consent given for operational security and maintenance cannot be converted into a license to impair the good. In Islamic jurisprudence, custodial authority delegated for maintenance and preservation (amânat al-hifz) strictly excludes any act of impairment (itlâf); exceeding delegated custodial boundaries constitutes unauthorized disposal in another's property (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr) and an actionable breach of trust. Mecelle Article 96 establishes that no one may dispose of another's property without permission, and Article 100 provides that whoever attempts to revoke what has been perfected on his part, his attempt is rejected.
The instance: at t one, X is functioning properly. M pushes a remote firmware change. B discovers that performance is throttled or third-party replacement batteries are blocked. M has directly invaded and impaired B's property.
The rule: M executed the code modification directly across the network, acting as the direct tortfeasor (mubâshir). Under Mecelle Article 92, the direct actor is strictly liable for property damage (itlâf) even without subjective malice. B is entitled to injunctive relief, specific performance—the full restoration of X to its warranted operating state—and compensatory damages for the incurred devaluation and lost utility.

MODEL THREE: THE CLOSING OF REPAIR.
The model: X suffers an ordinary mechanical or electronic wear fault. B, or an independent qualified technician, can repair it. The repair involves standard replacement parts and does not compromise biometric root keys or statutory emission safety, so any technical excuse T is either nonexistent or entirely disproportionate. M withholds diagnostic error codes, pairs parts through proprietary cryptographic serialization, or shuts down independent service tools.

OUR FIFTH EVIDENCE: VOID RESTRAINTS ON OWNERSHIP AND MANUFACTURED DISTRESS.
The claim: when M conditions the operation of X upon an absolute prohibition of external repair, M imposes a void restraint that contradicts the essence of ownership, manufactures artificial scarcity, and exploits the owner's necessitous state.
The proof has three steps. 
First, an absolute conveyance inherently transfers the unrestricted right of physical maintenance to the owner. A restraint imposed by M—whether by contractual clause, cryptographic parts-pairing, or software locks—prohibiting the owner from opening, repairing, or servicing his property directly contradicts the essence of sale (muqtadâ al-aqd). Under Islamic jurisprudence, a condition that strips the buyer of the essential prerogatives of ownership while securing an unearned monopoly for the maker is a corrupting condition (shart fâsid), rendering the restriction null and void ab initio. 
Second, when M monopolizes diagnostic tools and serialization keys, M manufactures artificial scarcity in essential maintenance. While classical market protection (ihtikâr) historically focused on grain, Imam Abu Yusuf broadened the doctrine to encompass any commodity or service whose deliberate withholding inflicts public hardship. Contemporary jurists, reasoning by systematic juristic extrapolation (takhrîj) from Abu Yusuf's foundational principle, extend this prohibition to essential repair parts, diagnostics, and serialization pairing whose monopolistic withholding inflicts systemic hardship (haraj) on the public. 
Third, by locking all independent repair doors, M forces the owner into a state of captive distress, compelling B to pay whatever extortionate price M dictates or prematurely discard X. Abu Dawud reports from Ali, may God be pleased with him, that the Prophet forbade the exploitative transaction forced upon a person in distress (bay' al-mudtarr).
The instance: an unbonded physical screen or sensor fails. M's serialization lock rejects an identical, certified replacement, forcing B to pay an extortionate fee at M's exclusive facility or discard X.
The rule: under Mecelle Article 100, M's attempt to restrict completed ownership is legally void. The restrictive lock is an unlawful impediment, and B has the untrammeled right to repair his property through any competent provider.

MODEL FOUR: THE SECTOR DYNAMICS.
The model: Models One, Two and Three expand from isolated corporate practices into an industry-wide cartel or convention. In this market, producers who implement concealed limits compete against producers who engineer durable goods. The model analyzes two successive phases of consumer and market reaction: the informational gap where repeat purchases occur, followed by the revelation of deceit and systemic collapse.

OUR SIXTH EVIDENCE: SYSTEMIC WASTE AND EXTERNALIZED DAMAGE TO THE COMMONS.
The claim: the private profit of premature replacement sales is captured by manufacturer M, while the externalized ecological degradation and material burdens are cast upon society and the commons.
The proof: in a linear or partially circular production model, truncating the operational lifespan of a good from E to L forces society to manufacture, transport, and dispose of goods at a vastly accelerated rate. Even accounting for modern industrial recycling, material recovery is inherently imperfect: substantial thermodynamic, chemical, and energetic losses occur, while toxic e-waste accumulates. The United Nations Global E-waste Monitor documented sixty-two million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022, less than a quarter of which was formally recycled, identifying shortened life cycles and restricted repairability as primary drivers. 
Regarding causal liability: B is merely the innocent last hand carrying the prematurely expired unit to the bin. M is the architect who engineered the premature end. When M degrades X remotely, M is the direct tortfeasor (mubâshir). When M designs X to fail prematurely at L, M is the causal actor (mutasabbib) who acted with intent. 
The maxims of the Mecelle govern this harm: a particular private harm is borne to repel a general public harm (Article 26); the greater harm is removed by the lesser (Article 27); and the burden follows the benefit (Mecelle Article 85: "Al-gurmu bi'l-gunm"). The squandering of planetary resources and the poisoning of the commons constitute an undeniable public harm (darar âmm). M's commercial desire for recurring sales cycles is merely a private interest. To prevent the public harm, the door of artificial obsolescence must be closed.
The instance: a population of N users requires durable utility over a timeline of twelve units. When E equals ten, replacement demand is minimal; when L is curtailed to four, the volume of extracted, processed, and discarded units multiplies, needlessly burdening the ecological commons.

OUR SEVENTH EVIDENCE: LEGISLATIVE WISDOM: ENMITY AND THE CORROSION OF TRUST.
The claim: the systemic practice of planned obsolescence breeds widespread social hostility and enmity once discovered, defeating the overarching legislative purpose of commercial ethics.
In juristic methodology, enmity is not the operative legal ground (illa) of the prohibition, because an illa must be an objective, measurable attribute. The operative ground is deceit and tortious damage (taghrîr ve itlâf), established in our first and second evidences. Enmity is the underlying legislative wisdom (hikma) of the prohibition. The Qur'an states in Surah Al-Ma'idah, verse 91: "Satan only seeks to sow enmity and hatred among you." The identical wisdom underpins the prohibition of commercial betrayal.
The proof: when a cyber-physical device perishes of natural wear, B harbors no grievance; he recognizes physical reality. But when investigative disclosure, source code leaks, or whistleblowing reveals that the failure was programmed by design or triggered by firmware, consumer perception transforms from ordinary trade into betrayal. The resulting friction erupts into collective litigation, consumer boycotts, and institutional cynicism.
The instance: independent security researchers expose the hidden limit L or the artificial throttling patch. The revelation destroys goodwill, replacing market trust with defensive hostility.
The rule: the prohibition stands firmly upon its operative grounds of deceit, latent defect, and delictual damage, and the eruption of public enmity confirms the legislative wisdom of the law.

OUR EIGHTH EVIDENCE: ADVERSE SELECTION AND THE DESTRUCTION OF HONEST TRADE.
The claim: during the period of information opacity—before consumers uncover the artificial limit—dishonest goods systematically drive durable, honest goods out of the marketplace.
The proof: consumers cannot directly verify the internal lifespan or uncorrupted design of a complex cyber-physical good at the point of retail sale. Prior to the widespread revelation described in Evidence Seven, market prices are formed based on observable external features. Because buyers cannot detect the hidden difference between E and L, an honest producer M1 who builds X for durability E cannot command a fair price premium. Meanwhile, producer M2 who limits X to L accelerates his repurchase cycle; uninformed buyers, attributing early failure to bad luck or battery fatigue, buy again. M2 captures outsized replacement revenues and aggressively expands marketing. The honest producer M1, receiving no premium for longevity and suffering longer sales intervals, is forced either to adopt artificial limits L or exit the market. A conventional warranty does not resolve this asymmetry: a warranty merely covers introductory failures, and M2 easily programs L to trigger shortly after the warranty expires. This dynamic represents classic adverse selection—the market for lemons—originating directly from the concealment of lifespan L.
The instance: two producers, M1 and M2, offer goods of identical outward appearance at price P. M1 builds for durability E; M2 programs limit L. Before the deceit is exposed, M2 extracts repetitive sales from unsuspecting buyers, gradually bankrupting M1 until only truncated goods survive on the market.
The rule: harm must be eliminated (Mecelle Article 19), and private gain cannot be tolerated when it destroys market integrity (Mecelle Article 26). Here the procedural duality of Islamic law resolves the tension between micro-contract remedies and macro-market order: while individual buyers utilize contractual options (hıyârât) against retailers S and product liability tort claims against makers M, prolonged societal use and widespread adoption can never convert a predatory practice into binding law. Under the foundational maxims of Islamic jurisprudence, a corrupt custom (al-'urf al-fâsid) that conflicts with clear legal texts and general welfare carries zero normative authority—even if it achieves universal industry prevalence. Consequently, the public regulatory authority (hisbah and siyâsah shar'iyyah) bears the non-delegable duty to intervene ex officio: invalidating collusive industry conventions, enforcing statutory transparency and minimum durability benchmarks, and penalizing engineered obsolescence to preserve authentic commerce and public trust.

THE JURISTIC ACCOUNTING OF GROUNDS AND SYSTEMIC HARMS.
To preserve precise methodology, the grounds are categorized into operative legal grounds (illal) and objective rational harms (mafâsid):

Six operative legal grounds (illal) derive from the texts, contract law, and the law of delict:
1. Eating wealth unjustly (ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl), in Evidence 1.
2. Gross disparity between price and value (gabn-i fâhiş), in Evidence 1.
3. Concealment and deceit (taghrîr and gharar), in Evidence 2.
4. Direct and causal property destruction (mubâşereten ve tesebbüben itlâf, Mecelle Articles 92 and 93), in Evidences 3 and 4.
5. Unauthorized disposal and abuse of custodial access (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr ve teaddî), in Evidence 4.
6. The corrupting restraint, artificial withholding, and exploitation of distress (shart fâsid, ihtikâr, and bay' al-mudtarr), in Evidence 5.

Four objective rational and systemic harms (mafâsid) derive from economic facts and legal maxims:
7. Unlawful preemption and premature appropriation of benefit (isticel, Mecelle Article 99), in Evidence 3.
8. Systemic misallocation of productive capital, in Evidence 3.
9. Ecological waste and public harm to the commons (darar âmm, Mecelle Article 26), in Evidence 6.
10. Systemic market collapse through adverse selection, in Evidence 8.

Enmity (adâvet) remains the overarching legislative wisdom (hikma) and is not counted among the operative causes. 
Under the higher objectives of Islamic law (Maqâsid al-Sharî'ah), planned obsolescence violates the protection of property (hifz al-mâl), commutative justice, social peace, and environmental balance.

NOW THE DEFENSES OF THE PRODUCERS.

The first defense: "The buyer accepted the end-user license agreement and service terms. Consent was given."
The refutation: in contract law, consent must be free, informed, and directed toward a lawful subject matter. 
First, if M openly declared on the box or at retail: "This good is designed for a rated life of four units of operational time, and its repair is locked," B's purchase would constitute valid consent to a truncated asset, and M would be vindicated—provided M does not subsequently trespass upon the good. But modern producers never make this disclosure; their business model relies on concealing L while charging the price of E.
Second, regarding adhesion clauses where B supposedly agrees that M may unilaterally degrade performance or brick functions remotely: such terms are legally void ab initio. In Islamic jurisprudence and modern unconscionability doctrine, a contractual term that negates the core objective of the contract (muqtadâ al-aqd) is a corrupting term (shart fâsid). A buyer cannot validly consent to the future sabotage of the very property he is purchasing. An adhesion clause buried in an electronic screen cannot legalize an unlawful tort or property destruction.

The second defense: "We had no choice. For battery stability, system integrity, or environmental compliance, we had to intervene and limit the good."
The refutation: this is the classic attempt to use a nominal technical factor T as an absolute immunity shield. But modern jurisprudence rejects the binary fallacy: T is governed by proportionality (al-tenâsüb) and the prohibition of abuse of rights (sû-i isti'mâl-i hak).
First, under Mecelle Article 22, "Necessities are measured by their true proportion" (Al-darûrâtü tütekkaderu bi-kaderihâ). When an engineering challenge arises—such as chemical battery aging causing voltage spikes, or new emission standards—a manufacturer cannot seize upon that technical reality as an excuse to impose permanent, unannounced performance throttling that coerces the owner into buying a replacement device. 
Second, necessity does not extinguish liability: Mecelle Article 33 establishes the timeless maxim: "Al-idtirâru lâ yubtilu haqqa'l-ghayr" (Necessity does not invalidate the right of another). Where an authentic, unavoidable safety hazard emerges, M is duty-bound to protect safety, but M cannot inflict uncompensated, permanent property devaluation upon B. Under Islamic product liability principles, M remains strictly bound to remedy the underlying defect: M must issue a transparent safety notification, offer the hardware repair or battery replacement at cost or free of charge under recall, and restore X to sound operating condition. Converting an engineering problem into a silent slowdown that manufactures replacement demand is not safety engineering; it is an unlawful evasion (hile-i şer'iyye) and commercial deceit masquerading as necessity.

The third defense: "We sold the physical hardware, but we retained the software. We licensed the code, and modern connected devices are dynamic cyber-physical services, not static physical chattel."
The refutation: this defense correctly recognizes that modern goods are cyber-physical, but reaches a legally perverse conclusion. Software and connectivity must be analyzed across three distinct juristic categories:
First, independent application software acquired separately: this can be legitimately licensed under copyright terms.
Second, embedded firmware and operating systems essential to the hardware's operation: without this code, X is reduced to an inert piece of metal. In jurisprudence, the accessory follows the principal: Mecelle Article 47 dictates that "what is subordinate to a thing in existence is subordinate to it in its legal ruling" (Al-tâbi'u tâbiun), and Article 48 states that "the subordinate cannot be given an independent ruling contrary to the principal." The cyber-physical good is the principal subject of sale; the integrated firmware is its indispensable attribute. The sale of X inherently conveys the permanent right to the functional utility of that embedded code. M retains intellectual property copyrights, but M cannot revoke, throttle, or weaponize the operational capacity of the sold property.
Third, connected network services and ongoing telemetry: connected devices require security patches against evolving threats. Here, ongoing remote access is legally categorized as a delegated custodial trust (amânat al-hifz). M is empowered to deploy defensive security patches; M is strictly forbidden from using this custodial channel to execute adverse property alterations. Furthermore, when M legitimately decides to end cloud support for legacy hardware, M has no right to brick the asset into a paperweight. M must ensure systemic separability: decoupling the physical hardware from the cloud, releasing local cryptographic authentication keys, and providing open local APIs so that the owner or independent communities can continue to operate and maintain the device. If M refuses this separation and insists on perpetual unilateral control, M must structure the transaction honestly as an ongoing lease (ijârah) and price it as rent, assuming the continuous upkeep liabilities of an owner-lessor.

The fourth defense: "Planned obsolescence finances innovation. Accelerated replacement cycles generate the revenue required for research and development, lowering hedonic unit costs and driving rapid technological progress."
The refutation: this defense fails on contractual, ethical, and economic grounds.
First, put it into the model. M secretly curtails X to L, withholding this from B, and justifies the premature replacement sale as financing the next generation of goods. B paid for E and received L. The value of the unreceived life was expropriated for an unagreed research fund. A noble end cannot sanctify an unlawful means (al-ghâyatu lâ tubarriru'l-wasîlah). If B were willing to fund future research through premature obsolescence, M would disclose it openly; M's concealment is proof that B would reject the bargain.
Second, hedonic price deflation—the real drop in computing cost per unit (such as Moore's Law)—is an authentic triumph of engineering, but it cannot justify artificial obsolescence. If the next-generation good is genuinely superior, consumers have an authentic incentive to upgrade voluntarily, without their existing property being artificially sabotaged. Hedonic progress thrives on voluntary adoption of better technology, not the forced destruction of existing utility. If older hardware cannot support the demanding computational requirements of newer software, M is fully entitled to freeze feature updates for that hardware, clearly announcing the end of support. Freezing feature enhancements preserves existing functionality without destroying the device. Genuine innovation elevates the new; planned obsolescence murders the old.

CONCLUSION.
The legal, rational, and moral ruling on planned obsolescence is definitively established wherever an artificial limit L is imposed without proportional necessity, or where a nominal technical pretext T is deployed to engineer unearned replacement sales:
First: to secretly engineer premature failure L into a durable good is to give short measure on the scale, an act of deceit, commutative fraud, and an actionable product liability tort.
Second: to remotely degrade a good after its sale, or to artificially lock its repair, is an unlawful trespass upon another's property, an abuse of custodial access, and a nullification of ownership.
Third: to force durable goods prematurely onto waste heaps generates severe public harm and squanders the earth's finite resources.
Fourth: to cloak these practices under the labels of license, safety, terms of service, or innovation does not diminish the wrong; it merely layers deceit upon harm.

For manufacturers, the boundary is clear and just:
If you produce economy goods, declare their rated operational lifespan (MTBF or cycle count) honestly and sell them openly.
If your hardware reaches its technical limit, declare the end of updates transparently, freeze the software in a stable state, and release local keys to preserve local autonomy.
If an authentic safety hazard arises, intervene openly, issue a recall, and repair the defect without cost to the owner under the doctrine of proportionality.
Either sell the good completely and relinquish your proprietary control, or lease it honestly and shoulder the burdens of maintenance. Any practice that extracts the purchase price of sale while exercising the remote power of guardianship is not trade; it is an unlawful imposition. Thank you.
