Today I will speak about planned obsolescence and its legal, rational and moral ruling.
I will not argue from objects. A machine, a part, a battery or a program carries its own physics, its own safety rules and its own laws, and the moment I name one of them, the argument turns into a debate about that thing and I lose the principle. So I will take the principle out of every object. In the language of the jurists this is the purification of the ground, tenkîh-i menât: I remove the brand, the model and the material, and I keep only what the ruling really depends on. What remains is a model with parties, a contract, ownership and a few variables. Every example in this talk is built only on this model and on its four forms.
Here are the variables. The seller is S. The buyer is B. The durable good that is sold is X. The price that B pays is P. The expected life of X, which nature, the science of materials and the custom of the market give it, is E. The limit that S puts on the life of X without telling B is L, and L is shorter than E. And there is one filter that I call the technical filter. It stands for every legitimate reason that an engineer, a safety rule or a law can give for a limit: protection of the good, protection of life, noise, weight, cost, an emission rule, a cyber-security need. I call it T, and I say T equals zero when no such reason exists.
The axiom is this: planned obsolescence, as an unlawful deceit and unlawful act, exists where T equals zero. Where the maker has a technical, safety or legal reason, T is not zero; that domain is the domain of necessity or technical boundary, and I answer the maker's claims regarding it in the second side of this talk. Where he has none, where the only difference between the good that lasts E and the good that ends at L is that the second one ends early, there is no engineering shelter for him to hide in. And because the maker holds the facts, the burden of naming the reason is on him. The Mecelle says the proof is on the claimant, and here the one who claims necessity or technical constraint is the maker. If he can name no reason, T is zero.
By planned obsolescence I mean a choice by the maker that shortens the useful life of a good he makes or sells, or takes away the owner's power to extend that life, where T is zero. The choice may be made before any sale, in the making of the good, or after the sale, by an act on it. Four things are outside this definition. First, ordinary wear, because nobody chose it. Second, a short life that is printed on the box or is the settled and known custom of that kind of goods, because the Mecelle says that what is known by custom is as if it were stipulated. Third, the real limit of the hardware: when a new program needs more than an old device has, the maker may decline to give the new feature and may declare the end of updates. Fourth, the loss of a function because an independent third party changed its own service, unless the maker built the good to depend on that service and did not say so. The maker may stop adding. He may end a service that he declared would end, with notice, leaving the good working or giving the owner what he needs to keep it alive. He may not kill.
The talk has two sides. On the first side stand our evidences, eight of them, built on four models. On the second side stand the evidences of the other side, the producers, four of them, and I will refute each one. Every evidence has the same shape: the claim, the proof that the effect follows from the cause, the instance in the model, and the rule.

MODEL ONE: THE LIMIT PLANTED BEFORE THE SALE.
Here is the model. S makes X. In making it, S has two technical choices, A one and A two. A one gives X the expected life E. A two gives X a life that ends at L. Between A one and A two there is no difference in cost, weight, noise, safety or law, so T is zero. S chooses A two only so that X ends early and the second sale comes sooner. B, at the time of the sale, which I call t zero, looks at the market and at custom, expects E, and pays P. To have numbers, let E be ten units of time and L be four. P was set for ten.

OUR FIRST EVIDENCE: THE PRICE BUYS SOMETHING THE BUYER DOES NOT RECEIVE.
The claim: a sale is sound only when what the buyer pays is matched by what he receives, and the part of the price that has no counter-value is taken without right. The proof has four steps. The price is set by what the buyer expects, and for a durable good that includes its ordinary life. At t zero S already knows the limit he chose, and B cannot see it. B pays for ten units and receives four. So a part of the price has no counter-value, and that part is the use value of the six missing units. Value is not spread evenly over the units of life, so I do not say that six tenths of the price is the exact share. The exact share is set by those who know the trade, as the jurists set value by the experts of the trade. What is established is that a part exists, and that it belongs to B.
The Qur'an says in Surah An-Nisa, verse 29: "Do not consume your wealth among yourselves by false ways." Taking a price with no counter-value is eating property by a false way, ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl. The jurists also treat a gross gap between price and value, gabn-i fâhiş, and they measure it at the time of the contract. In the model the gap exists at t zero, because the limit was planted before the sale. The schools accept small gaps and draw the line between a small gap and a gross one anywhere from a twentieth of the value to a third. Six units out of ten is beyond every line that any school has drawn.
The rule: when a sale is accompanied by fraudulent concealment and deceit (taghrir) causing a gross gap between price and value (gabn-i fâhiş), the contract is corrupted on the part of the deceiver. In the Hanafi view that Mecelle Article 356 and 357 codify, the deceived buyer B has the right to rescind the contract entirely and recover P, returning the defective good. If the good cannot be returned due to loss, destruction or substantial change, compensation for the missing value is due. The Hanbali school gives B a direct choice between rescinding the sale or keeping X with restitution of the excess price paid without counter-value. In both schools, the seller S has no lawful right to retain the unearned price against B's will.

OUR SECOND EVIDENCE: A CONCEALED ATTRIBUTE AND DECEIT.
The claim: when the seller hides an essential attribute that sets the price, the contract is vitiated by gharar and by taghrir. The proof has four steps. The expected durable life of X is an implied quality (vasf) that sets its price. S alone knows L, and B has no means of discovering it at t zero. A seller who stays silent where speaking was needed makes a fraudulent misrepresentation by his silence, and the statement is that the good conforms to the customary standard. So B gives his consent on a false picture, and S made the false picture. The texts agree. The Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade the transaction corrupted by gharar, which Muslim reports. He also said: whoever deceives us is not one of us. He found wet grain under dry grain in a heap, and he asked the seller why he had not put it on top, so that people could see it. The buyer could not have found the wet grain himself, and the seller was still blamed. L is the wet grain at the bottom of the heap. In Hanafi jurisprudence, while general ignorance of the goods that leads to dispute makes a contract fasid, a latent defect or concealed deficiency in an identified object renders the sale non-binding (ghayr lazim), granting the buyer the full option of defect.
The instance: B can look at X as long as he wishes and will never find the number four. The number is in S's plan, and S says nothing.
The rule: because L was planted before the sale, it is a latent defect (ayb-i hafî) hidden at t zero. B holds the option of defect (khiyâr al-ayb). He may rescind the sale and recover P in full, or retain X if he so chooses, and where restitution of the good is barred, he is entitled to financial compensation (nuqsân-ı arz). If L had not been planted at t zero and were imposed later by remote interference, the matter would not be an original defect of sale; it would be an unauthorized act against another's property, governed by Model Two.

OUR THIRD EVIDENCE: HARM DONE ON PURPOSE, AND THE GAIN THAT COMES FROM IT.
The claim: nobody may cause harm on purpose and then profit from it. The proof has five steps. Natural wear comes from the fatigue of materials, and nobody designs it. Here S's choice goes the other way: it is made so that X fails at L. Remove that choice, and B keeps X for ten units while S loses the second sale. So S's gain comes directly from B's loss. The loss is real, because B pays again at four units, six units before he planned to. A limit for which S can name no protective purpose has no lawful benefit. If a protective purpose exists, T is not zero, and S states it and tells B. And the harm has a second face. The price that B pays prematurely cannot be spent anywhere else. This is not stagnation of capital. It is misallocation of capital: resources that would have gone to productive investment, welfare or savings are forced into replacing what should not have failed.
The rules are two. The Mecelle says there is no harm and no answering harm with harm, and harm is removed (Articles 19 and 20). Nobody can obtain a lawful gain from a harm that he caused by his own intentional and unjust act. The second rule, Mecelle Article 99, says: "Men iste'cele şey'en kable evânihî ûkıbe bi-hırmânih." Whoever hastens something before its time by an illegitimate means is deprived of it (isticel). The classical case is the heir who kills his benefactor to inherit early; the law deprives him of the inheritance. I use the rule by strict juristic analogy. The underlying legal reason (illa) is hastening an unearned benefit before its due time by an illegitimate means, and that reason is fully present when S artificially brings the second replacement sale from ten units to four. B has a lawful claim to recover from S what this premature obsolescence cost him. S, as the one whose planning caused the failure, is a causer (mutasabbib), and Article 93 says that a causer is liable when he acted intentionally. The word planned supplies the intent.
The instance: at four units, B is forced to replace X. The replacement was due after ten units, and S has artificially moved the second purchase six units forward.

MODEL TWO: THE ACT ON THE GOOD AFTER THE SALE.
The model: the sale is complete at t zero, and full ownership and possession are wholly with B. No danger to life, no safety law and no security need arises, so T is zero. X does everything that was delivered. At a later time t one, S uses a remote channel that he retained, and he removes a function of X, or lowers its performance, or makes X reject parts that the owner installs to maintain it.

OUR FOURTH EVIDENCE: THE OWNER'S PROPERTY IS ACTED ON FROM AFAR.
The claim: when the sale is complete, S's power of disposal over X ends entirely, and any unilateral adverse act he executes on X afterwards is an unauthorized disposal in another man's property. The proof: a valid sale transfers complete ownership to B, and ownership entails the full exclusive right to use, alter, maintain and preserve the good. If S claims that B agreed to remote software updates at initial setup, that consent was given for maintenance, optimization and security; in the law of contract, a general consent for beneficial maintenance cannot be construed as a license to impair or degrade the good. Using a maintenance permission to inflict latent diminution of utility is an abuse of right and a deviation from the contract's object. The Mecelle says that nobody may act in another man's property without his permission, and nobody may take another man's property without a lawful cause, Articles 96 and 97. It also says that whoever tries to undo what is complete on his own side, his effort is rejected, Article 100. S's act is an unauthorized disposal (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr) and an unlawful trespass.
The instance: at t one, X is working as delivered. S transmits a remote change. B can no longer use the standard replacement part or feature, and the operational capability that the sale gave him is diminished by S's direct intervention.
The rule: S acted directly upon X through code execution, so he is the direct actor (mubâshir), and the direct actor is strictly liable even without subjective intent, as Article 92 establishes. B may demand that the damaging modification be reversed and may claim compensation for all diminution in value and costs incurred. If preserving X as it was had presented an imminent safety hazard, T would not be zero; S would have been justified to intervene, provided he acted transparently and provided a prompt remedy. But he may not degrade property in secret for commercial advantage.

MODEL THREE: THE REPAIR IS CLOSED.
The model: X has an ordinary fault. B, or an independent expert, can fix it. B asks for nothing that belongs to S's copyright, patent or trade secret. He asks only for the diagnostic error information and standard repair access, and the replacement part meets every technical standard, so T is zero. S withholds the error information, or locks X with cryptographic pairing that rejects anything that did not come from S's own exclusive supply.

OUR FIFTH EVIDENCE: THE REPAIR IS CLOSED AND THE OWNER HAS ONE DOOR.
The claim: when S reserves the means of repair exclusively to himself, he imposes an invalid condition that contradicts the essence of ownership, creates an artificial scarcity, and exploits the owner's necessitous condition. The proof has three steps. First, sale transfers full proprietorship to B, which inherently includes the right to repair and maintain the property. A condition imposed by S—whether express or embedded in hardware—that dictates "you may not repair this good, you may not take it to a qualified third party, you may not install compatible parts," directly contradicts the contractual essence of ownership (muqtadâ al-aqd). The jurists term such a condition a corrupting condition (shart fâsid); it is legally void, because S pockets the full price of ownership while withholding the essential prerogatives of ownership. Second, when diagnostic data and repair keys are withheld from the owner and confined solely to S's authorized network, the scarcity of repair is artificially manufactured by S. While classical ihtikâr refers to hoarding food staples from the market, leading jurists, including Abu Yusuf, extend the prohibition of ihtikâr to any artificial withholding of goods or essential services that causes public hardship (haraj). The refusal to supply repair access coupled with a restrictive lock produces this exact unlawful harm. Third, when the only party permitted to repair is the original seller, the owner is placed in a captive state of distress, forced to accept whatever exorbitant price S dictates. A report from Ali, may God be pleased with him, records that the Prophet forbade the exploitative transaction forced upon a person in distress (bay' al-mudtarr), reported by Abu Dawud.
In this model, B asks for no proprietary blueprints, source code or trade secrets. S retains his intellectual property intact. B demands only fair, standard diagnostic access and unencumbered pairing, which are the natural incidents of physical ownership.
The instance: the physical fault costs one unit of effort and materials to remedy. S's monopolized gate costs B ten units, and S has locked all other doors.
The rule: the seller cannot undo the completed effect of sale by locking the property against its owner; his attempt is legally void under Mecelle Article 100. The restrictive condition is null, and B has the unimpeded legal right to repair his own property through any competent provider.

MODEL FOUR: THE SECTOR DYNAMICS.
The model: Models One, Two and Three evolve from isolated practices into an industry-wide dynamic. In this sector, producers who adopt planned obsolescence compete against those who build durable goods. The natural replacement of X due to shifting consumer preference, or genuine technological breakthroughs that render older goods obsolete by superior performance, are strictly excluded from this model. Only replacement forced by artificial failure, degradation, or closed repair belongs to it.

OUR SIXTH EVIDENCE: WASTE AND THE BURDEN PUT ON THE COMMONS.
The claim: the private gain of premature replacement sales is pocketed by S, while the externalized ecological and resource costs are cast onto the public commons. The proof is mathematical. To deliver a given quantity of operational service over time, a good truncated to life L must be manufactured, transported, and discarded E over L times as often as a good built for life E. Let E be six units of time and L be three. The physical volume of goods manufactured and discarded exactly doubles. Each extra unit consumes virgin materials, energy, and disposal capacity. These externalized damages do not appear on S's invoice, yet they fall squarely upon society and the environment. The United Nations Global E-waste Monitor documented sixty-two million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022, finding that less than a quarter was properly collected and recycled, citing shorter product life cycles, restricted repairability, and design barriers as primary drivers. The arithmetic applies strictly to goods replaced because they were artificially disabled or broken.
Regarding causal liability: B is merely the final hand discarding the expired unit. S is the designer who engineered the premature end or disabled the good. When S disables X remotely, he is the direct actor (mubâshir). When X fails by design, S is the causal actor (mutasabbib) who acted with deliberate intent. In both instances, legal liability rests on S.
The governing principles of the Mecelle are unambiguous: a private harm is endured to avert a public harm (Article 26); the greater harm is removed by the lesser (Article 27); and the burden accompanies the benefit (Article 87). The depletion of finite resources and the accumulation of toxic waste constitute an undeniable public harm (darar amm). S's commercial desire for repetitive sales is merely a private benefit (darar khass). To avert the public harm, the door to artificial obsolescence must be closed.
The instance: N households each require utility X for twelve units of time. With E equal to six, each requires two units. With L equal to three, each must consume four units. The sector unnecessarily consumes and discards 2N additional units of goods, doubling the societal burden.

OUR SEVENTH EVIDENCE: THE WISDOM OF THE BAN IS ENMITY, AND THE PRACTICE BREEDS IT.
The claim: the practice systematically generates the societal enmity and distrust that the divine prohibition of deceit exists to prevent. In legal theory, enmity is not the operative ground (illa) of the ruling, because an illa must be an objective, measurable attribute, whereas an emotion is internal. The operative ground is deceit (taghrir and khiyana), established in the second evidence. Enmity is the underlying legislative wisdom (hikma) behind the prohibition. The Qur'an states in Surah Al-Ma'idah, verse 91, regarding the prohibition of intoxicants and gambling: "Satan only seeks to sow enmity and hatred among you." The identical rationale underpins the prohibition of commercial cheating: preserving trust and peace within the community.
The proof: when a durable good ends by natural wear, B harbors no grievance; he recognizes natural material limits and accepts the outcome. But when B discovers that the termination was premeditated by design or firmware, his perception of S transforms from a trading partner into an adversary. This generates measurable societal friction: widespread disputes, grievances, and collective litigation.
The instance: within the sector, buyer B1 discovers the hidden limit L. He alerts B2, and the knowledge spreads to B3. Collective distrust erupts, market cohesion dissolves, and peaceful commerce is replaced by adversarial hostility.
The rule: the prohibition stands firmly upon its legal ground of deceit, and the visible manifestation of enmity validates the divine wisdom of the law.

OUR EIGHTH EVIDENCE: ADVERSE SELECTION AND THE COLLAPSE OF MARKET INTEGRITY.
The claim: when artificial obsolescence spreads without transparent disclosure, dishonest practices systematically drive honest, durable goods out of the marketplace. The proof has five steps. Consumers cannot inspect the latent design lifespan of a complex durable good at the time of purchase. Market prices are formed predominantly on observable exterior attributes. Consequently, an honest producer who builds a good for life E at higher engineering cost cannot command a fair premium, because the buyer cannot verify the hidden longevity. Conversely, a producer who engineers an early limit L incurs lower manufacturing costs or accelerates repurchase revenue. He can undercut the honest producer's price or achieve excessive margins. The honest producer, facing uncompensated durability costs, is forced either to lower his standards to L or exit the market altogether. A commercial warranty does not resolve this information asymmetry; a warranty merely covers interim repair for a stipulated short duration and reveals nothing about total product longevity. The seller who plans the end easily sets L just beyond the warranty threshold. This dynamic represents classic adverse selection—the "market for lemons"—originating directly from the manufacturer's concealment of lifespan L.
The instance: two producers, S one and S two, present competing goods on the market shelf at price P. S one builds for durability E, while S two secretly designs for premature failure L. S two captures higher returns or undercuts price, gradually compelling S one to abandon durability or go bankrupt, leaving consumers with an all-lemon market.
The rule: harm must be eliminated, and private gain cannot be tolerated at the expense of market collapse (Articles 20 and 26). The public supervisory authority (hisbah / market regulatory body) has a strict legal obligation to intervene, mandate durability disclosure, and prohibit concealed truncation to preserve fair competition and commercial honesty.

THE GROUNDS ACCOUNTED FOR.
The legal grounds (illal) established across the eight evidences total ten distinct causes, distributed rigorously:
Six grounds derive from the sacred texts and classical contract law:
1. Eating wealth unjustly (ekl-i emvâl bi'l-bâtıl), in Evidence 1.
2. Gross disparity between price and value (gabn-i fâhiş), in Evidence 1.
3. Concealment and deceit (taghrir and gharar), in Evidence 2.
4. Causing intentional harm (dırâr), in Evidence 3.
5. Unauthorized disposal in another's property (tasarruf fî milk al-ghayr), in Evidence 4.
6. The corrupting condition and artificial withholding (shart fâsid and the harm of ihtikâr), in Evidence 5.

Four grounds derive from reason, systemic facts, and legal principles:
7. Unlawful preemption and premature appropriation of benefit (isticel, Mecelle Article 99), in Evidence 3.
8. Severe misallocation of productive capital, in Evidence 3.
9. Ecological waste and public harm to the commons (darar amm), in Evidence 6.
10. Systemic market degradation through adverse selection, in Evidence 8.

Enmity (adâvet) is maintained as the underlying legislative wisdom (hikma) and is not counted among the operative grounds.
Examined from the objectives of Islamic jurisprudence (Maqasid al-Shariah), these grounds violate the protection of property (hifz al-mal), contractual integrity, social peace, and ecological balance. Planned obsolescence is not an ordinary commercial maneuver of a free market; it is a compounded contractual wrong.

NOW THE EVIDENCES OF THE OTHER SIDE.
The first defense of the producers: "The buyer accepted the terms of service and end-user license. He gave his consent."
The refutation: consent in contract law must be enlightened, voluntary, and aligned with the essential purpose of the transaction. First, if explicit terms detailing the product's truncated lifespan L or unilateral degradation were clearly displayed prior to purchase, with full opportunity for B to reject without detriment, consent would be valid. That is not what occurs. Second, our refutation addresses the deceptive consent: adhesion terms presented only after P has been paid, ambiguous software agreements that mask subsequent utility reductions, and mandatory updates presented where refusal bricks the device. When B accepts a routine update presented as a "security patch," he consents to maintenance; he does not consent to the throttling of his processor or the intentional disabling of his battery. Under Islamic jurisprudence, consent obtained through misrepresentation or concealment is legally defective (rida ghayr sahîh). Conditions that negate the fundamental objective of the sale are null and void, and a forced electronic signature cannot substitute for genuine contractual consent.

The second defense: "We had no choice. For safety, stability, or legal compliance, we had to limit or retire the good."
The refutation: this is the precise case where T is not zero, which lies entirely outside the definition of planned obsolescence. Where a genuine, demonstrable technical or safety necessity emerges, the law recognizes the excuse. However, necessity does not extinguish liability. Mecelle Article 33 establishes the timeless maxim: "Necessity does not invalidate the right of another" (Al-idtirâru lâ yubtilu haqqa'l-ghayr). The classical precedent is the starving traveler who consumes another's bread to preserve life: he is legally permitted to eat it, yet he remains strictly obligated to pay for its value afterwards. Therefore, when S genuinely discovers an unexpected safety hazard in X, S possesses the right and duty to intervene immediately to protect life. But S cannot use safety as a pretext to confiscate utility in secret. S must disclose the hazard transparently, provide an effective remedy, and make the owner whole for any unearned loss of value. Genuine technical necessity requires disclosure and restitution; silent degradation without remedy is an offense, not an engineering necessity.

The third defense: "We sold the physical hardware, but we retained the software. We licensed the code, and we are free to modify or terminate it."
The refutation: software must be analyzed according to its juristic reality across three distinct categories:
First, independent application software acquired separately and installed by the user on his own discretion: this can be legitimately licensed under copyright terms.
Second, embedded firmware and operating systems essential to the hardware's operation: without this code, X is reduced to an inert piece of metal. In jurisprudence, the accessory follows the principal: Mecelle Article 47 dictates that "what is subordinate to a thing in existence is subordinate to it in its legal ruling" (Al-tâbi'u tâbiun), and Article 48 states that "the subordinate cannot be given an independent ruling contrary to the principal." The hardware is the principal subject of sale; the integrated software is its indispensable attribute. The sale of X inherently conveys the right to the continuous, unhindered use of that embedded code. S retains copyright and patent rights, but S cannot revoke the operational soul of the purchased property.
Third, remote cloud services hosted on S's infrastructure: here S legitimately asserts that the server infrastructure is his own. If an ongoing cloud service was promised at the time of sale, S is contractually bound to maintain it for the duration represented. When that term ends, S may terminate the cloud service with reasonable advance notice; however, S must leave the local physical good functional, or provide open local interfaces enabling the owner to operate X autonomously. In contracts, legal regard is paid to true meaning and intent, not superficial phrasing (Mecelle Article 3). If S intends merely to rent utility, he must explicitly frame the contract as a lease (ijârah) and price it as rent, assuming the lessor's ongoing maintenance burdens.

The fourth defense: "Planned obsolescence finances innovation. Accelerated replacement cycles generate the revenue required for research and development, lowering hedonic unit costs and driving rapid technological progress."
The refutation: this defense fails on contractual, ethical, and economic grounds.
First, put it into the model. S secretly curtails X to L, withholding this from B, and justifies the premature replacement sale as financing the next generation of goods. B paid for E and received L. The value of the unreceived life was expropriated for an unagreed research fund. A noble end cannot sanctify an unlawful means (al-ghâyatu lâ tubarriru'l-wasîlah). If B were willing to fund future research through premature obsolescence, S would disclose it openly; S's concealment is proof that B would reject the bargain.
Second, hedonic price deflation—the real drop in computing cost per unit (such as Moore's Law)—is an authentic triumph of engineering, but it cannot justify artificial obsolescence. If the next-generation good is genuinely superior, consumers have an authentic incentive to upgrade voluntarily, without their existing property being artificially sabotaged. Hedonic progress thrives on voluntary adoption of better technology, not the forced destruction of existing utility. If older hardware cannot support the demanding computational requirements of newer software, S is fully entitled to freeze feature updates for that hardware, clearly announcing the end of support. Freezing feature enhancements preserves existing functionality without destroying the device. Genuine innovation elevates the new; planned obsolescence murders the old.

CONCLUSION.
The legal, rational, and moral ruling on planned obsolescence is definitively established wherever T equals zero:
First: to secretly engineer premature failure L into a durable good is to give short measure on the scale, an act of deceit and unlawful appropriation of wealth.
Second: to remotely degrade a good after its sale, or to artificially lock its repair, is an unlawful trespass upon another's property and a nullification of ownership.
Third: to force durable goods prematurely onto waste heaps generates severe public harm and squanders the earth's finite resources.
Fourth: to cloak these practices under the labels of license, safety, terms of service, or innovation does not diminish the wrong; it merely layers deceit upon harm.
For manufacturers, the boundary is clear and just:
If you produce economy goods, state their expected life honestly and sell them openly.
If your hardware reaches its technical limit, declare the end of updates transparently and freeze the software in a stable, working state.
If an authentic safety hazard arises, intervene openly, provide the remedy, and make the owner whole.
Relinquish control over goods you have sold, honor the owner's right to repair, and compete through authentic technological excellence. Any practice that transgresses these boundaries is not trade; it is exploitation.
