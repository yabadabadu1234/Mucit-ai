Planned Obsolescence

A ruling, its proof, and why no defense survives it.
Thesis. A maker may not shorten the useful life of a product he has sold, or take away its owner’s power to keep it working, without the buyer’s knowledge and consent. Doing so is unjust, and the classical rulings against it are sound.
Beşir Kerem Üstüntürk · 251201037 · October 2026
Orientation

What this talk will do

  • Define the claim exactly, and say what it leaves out.
  • Show how a moral ruling is proven, step by step, so you can check each step yourself.
  • Prove the ruling from independent grounds: consent, harm, ownership. A fourth ground, waste, is offered as support only.
  • Close every escape route with a complete yes/no tree of defenses, then meet the maker’s own case (“it funds innovation”) with five logical tests.
  • Test the ruling on four public cases and twenty-eight doubts.
  • State the ruling in force: what is forbidden, what is required, what is permitted.
How certain is each step?

CERTAIN every premise is certain and the form is valid.

STRONG sound texts or clear reasoning; a minority objection can be stated.

PROBABLE analogy, or a point scholars dispute; the better view is adopted and named.

SUPPORT ONLY adds weight; the proof does not depend on it.

Rule of the weakest link: a conclusion is never stronger than the weakest step that leads to it.

The starting point

Start with one purchase

You pay a price P for a device. You expect it to serve you for a time E, as devices of its kind ordinarily do. The maker knows, from a design or software decision he took, that it will serve for only L, and L is less than E. Nobody tells you.

Shortfall = P × (1 − L / E)

If the device serves half as long as expected, half of the price bought nothing. The formula is only arithmetic: P, E and L are variables, and this talk claims no numbers for them.

Did you consent to pay for E?
Yes. You paid because you believed in it.
Did you receive what you paid for?
No. You received L.
Who could have prevented the gap at no cost?
The one who knew: the maker.
Planned Obsolescence3 / 62
The claim

What exactly is planned obsolescence?

Definition. Planned obsolescence is a choice, made by the maker, that shortens the useful life of a product he has sold, or removes its owner’s power to extend that life.
Three words that matter
  • Choice. An accident, or a flaw the maker did not know of, is not planned.
  • Maker. The one who designs, sells, updates, supports or controls the parts and tools.
  • Useful life. Durability plus maintainability: how long it works, and how long its owner can keep it working.
What the claim leaves out
  • Natural wear and accident: time, not a choice, ends the life.
  • Real progress: a better new device leaves the old one unchanged.
  • Changing taste: fashion alters desire, not the old device.
  • An honest short life: a product whose short life the buyer knows about when he buys it.

The method

How a moral ruling is proven here

  1. Name the act exactly. (Done: the map.)
  2. State the norms the ruling rests on, each with a reason anyone can check.
  3. Show where the classical jurists ruled on the same norm, with their texts and the grade of each text.
  4. Apply the norms to each leaf and state the conditions under which they bind.
  5. Close the proof: list every possible defense in a complete yes/no tree and answer each.
  6. Mark the certainty of each step. The weakest step sets the strength of the whole.
Why reasons come first

A reader who does not accept a text cannot be moved by it. He can be moved by a premise he already holds.

So each norm is first shown to be one that its denier would have to give up everything else to refuse. The classical texts then confirm the norm and fix its details.

What a classical jurist does with a new case

He finds the decided case that shares the cause (ʿilla) of the ruling, and extends the ruling by analogy (qiyās). A new case needs no new rule, only the old reason.

The proof at a glance

Three facts, three norms, one ruling

Facts of the case
F1 The maker knows the limit he put on the product’s life or repair.
F2 The buyer does not know it.
F3 The price was set for the life the buyer ordinarily expects.
Norms
N1 Consent. Wealth passes from one person to another only by informed consent.
N2 Harm. No one may gain by another’s loss without a right.
N3 Ownership. The owner alone governs use, repair and disposal.
N4 Waste. Usable value may not be destroyed for no benefit. (support only)
Rulings
R1 Concealing the limit is forbidden.
R2 Changing or disabling a sold device, or blocking its repair, without informed consent is forbidden.
R3 The one who knows the limit must disclose it.
R4 The wronged buyer has a remedy.

Where F1 to F3 are absent, there is no planned obsolescence and the ruling does not apply. The proof is conditional on the facts, and the facts are checked case by case.

Principle 1 · Consent

A “yes” needs knowledge behind it

Imagine a baker who mixes an ingredient, not on the label, into his bread so that it goes stale in a day. You paid for bread that keeps. Tomorrow you must buy again. Would anyone call that a fair sale?
The norm
N1. Wealth passes from one person to another only by informed consent.

“Informed” means the buyer knew the facts that set the price, or could not have missed them.

Try to deny it
  • Then a seller may promise one thing and hand over another, and the payment still counts as agreed.
  • Then a price tells the buyer nothing about what he gets.
  • Then no one could rationally pay in advance for anything.

So N1 is not an extra rule laid on trade. It is what makes a price mean something. Whoever denies it has already stopped trading.

Principle 1 · Consent

Proof: concealing the limit takes part of the price without consent

  1. A buyer pays a price because he believes the product will give him a benefit for a time E.
  2. The maker knows the product will give that benefit only for L, and L < E. (fact F1)
  3. The buyer formed E from what such products ordinarily give, and from the maker’s silence. (F2, F3)
  4. So the buyer agreed to pay for E. He did not agree to pay for L. (from 1.1, 1.3)
  5. Wealth taken beyond what was agreed is taken wrongly. (N1; Qur’an 4:29)
  6. Therefore the part P (1 − L/E) was taken wrongly. CERTAIN

Principle 1 · Consent

What the jurists and the Mecelle add

The rulings of the schools
  • Gain obtained by tadlīs or ghish (hiding a defect, or showing a quality that is not there) is forbidden gain. (TDV, “Tağrîr”)
  • The deceived buyer may rescind (Mālikīs, Shāfiʿīs, Ḥanbalīs and others). Abū Ḥanīfa and Muḥammad prefer compensation for the loss. (TDV, “Hile”)
  • The ruler may punish the deceiver by taʿzīr; scholars agree on this. (TDV, “Hile”)
  • A defect (ʿayb) is what lowers the value of the goods in the eyes of the experts (Mecelle 338). The Shāfiʿīs also count a shortfall that frustrates the buyer’s legitimate purpose. (TDV, “Ayıp”)
The Mecelle’s maxims on the same point
  • Art. 67. No statement is attributed to a silent person, but silence at the place where need calls for speech is a statement.
  • Art. 43–44. What is known by custom is as if stipulated; what is known among merchants binds them as if agreed.
  • Art. 3. In contracts, meaning counts, not wording.
A hidden limit on life lowers the value in expert eyes, frustrates the purpose of buying, and is silence where speech was needed. It meets all three tests.
Mecelle article numbers and wording: Ali Hikmet Berki’s annotated edition as reproduced by M. Balcı; art. 338 and the ayb rules: TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, “Ayıp” (md. 336–355).

Principle 1 · Consent

From the udder to the device: the classical analogy

The decided case (aṣl)

A seller keeps an animal unmilked for days so that its udder looks full. The Prophet (peace be upon him) forbade it; the buyer may milk, then keep the animal or return it. Sahih al-Bukhārī 2148; Sahih Muslim 1515

The cause (ʿilla)

The seller creates a false appearance of a quality that sets the price, and the buyer relies on it.

The new case (farʿ)

The maker hides a limit on the product’s life; the price was set for a life the product does not have, and the buyer relies on the silence.

Ruling carried over: forbidden, with the buyer’s option to rescind or be compensated.
  • What the udder hides is yield. What the design hides is life. Both are qualities the buyer pays for and cannot see.
  • The texts forbid the appearance, not the animal. The cause is therefore not special to livestock.
  • No new principle is introduced. The old principle meets a new object.

STRONG Analogy from sound texts, plus the general texts on the previous slides.

Principle 1 · Consent

Where the principle stops, and the best objection

Boundaries: no concealment where
  • The limit was disclosed in plain words, before the sale, where a buyer would see it.
  • The buyer knew it and accepted it. He then has no option to rescind. (TDV, “Ayıp”) A limit any ordinary buyer would see at once is not hidden.
  • The limit is the known norm of that kind of product, so no ordinary buyer expects more (Mecelle 41–42: custom counts only when it is settled and prevailing).

Conditions that must hold for the ruling to bind: the maker knew; the buyer did not; the shortfall is material; the buyer relied on the ordinary expectation.

Objection

“The buyer could have asked. It is his duty to check.”

Reply

Where the matter is one the buyer needs to know, silence is itself a statement (Mecelle 67). And in the grain-heap case the owner is blamed for not showing the wet grain although the buyer could not see it. The duty lies on the one who knows, and a hidden limit is exactly what the buyer cannot find by looking.

Principle 1 · Consent

Two more grounds inside consent: equivalence and uncertainty

Equivalence in exchange
  • A sale swaps two counter-values. The shortfall formula measures how far they have come apart.
  • The jurists do not demand exact equality. A small disparity (gabn-i yesîr) does not touch the contract; a gross one (gabn-i fâhiş) is judged by the custom of that trade.
  • Hanafīs and Shāfiʿīs give the buyer an option to rescind for gross disparity only where there is deceit (taghrīr), as Mecelle 356 follows. Mālikīs, Ḥanbalīs and others give it even without deceit.
  • All agree that a buyer who knowingly accepts the disparity cannot rescind.
Here the strictest school is met: the disparity is hidden, which is deceit. The claim also needs the shortfall to be material, so a trivial gap is outside it.
Uncertainty (gharar)
  • The Prophet forbade a sale that contains gharar, uncertainty about what is being sold (Sahih Muslim 1513, Abū Hurayra).
  • Classical gharar is uncertainty about the subject itself: existence, amount or attribute. Light uncertainty that people tolerate does not void a sale.
  • Here the unknown attribute is the life, which sets the price. The seller knows it and the buyer does not. This is the lopsided form of gharar, which the jurists treat as taghrīr.

STRONG Equivalence: clear, with the school difference stated.
PROBABLE Gharar: the text is sound; fitting a one-sided hidden attribute under it is our step. We therefore do not rest N1 on it.

Gabn and taghrīr: TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, “Gabn” (page opened). Muslim 1513: wording from search summaries of tohed.com; the page itself was not opened.

Principle 2 · Harm

Gain may not be bought with another’s loss

Imagine a factory owner who saves money by letting his waste run into his neighbors’ wells. The land is his and the profit is his. Does that make it right?
The norm
N2. No one may gain by another’s loss without a right to do so.

A “right” here is a ground that survives questioning: the other’s informed consent, a real necessity, or an entitlement that does not itself take another’s right. The defense tree later lists all of them.

Why it applies here
  • The buyer loses the part of the price that bought nothing (Principle 1).
  • He also loses time and effort: he must buy again, earlier than he planned.
  • The maker’s gain comes from that loss: the sale is brought forward, or the repair market is kept.

The test of connection: remove the maker’s choice. If the buyer would not have lost, and the maker would not have gained, the harm is the maker’s doing.

Planned Obsolescence17 / 62
Principle 2 · Harm

Proof: the harm is real, caused, and without a right

  1. The usable life of a product the buyer owns has value to him. (he paid for it)
  2. A choice by the maker removes part of that life, or part of the owner’s power to keep it. (definition; F1)
  3. But for that choice the owner would have kept it. (test of connection)
  4. The owner did not consent to the loss. (F2, Principle 1)
  5. The maker gains, or at least keeps, a market that the loss opens or protects. (replacement, repair monopoly)
  6. Absent consent, necessity or an entitlement, a gain made this way is without right. (N2; defenses tested later)
  7. Therefore the choice is harm, and harm is to be removed and made good. STRONG

Step 2.5 is not needed for the wrong itself: a cost-cutting maker who gains nothing from the shortened life still harms. It is needed only to answer the claim that the harm is a by-product.

Strong rather than certain because step 2.6 depends on the defense tree on later slides: the tree has to hold.

Principle 2 · Harm

What the classical texts and the Mecelle say

The text

لَا ضَرَرَ وَلَا ضِرَارَ

There is to be neither harming nor reciprocating harm.

  • Sunan Ibn Mājah 2340 (ʿUbāda ibn al-Ṣāmit) and 2341 (Ibn ʿAbbās); Mālik, al-Muwaṭṭaʾ; al-Nawawī, Forty Ḥadīth no. 32 (Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī).
  • Grade, stated honestly: al-Nawawī calls it ḥasan through several chains. al-Albānī calls both ṣaḥīḥ; Shuʿayb al-Arnaʾūṭ calls both ṣaḥīḥ li-ghayrihi; Darussalam and Zubair ʿAlī Zaʾī call both weak.
  • The Qur’an rejects harm of this shape elsewhere: “do not retain them to harm them” (2:231).
The Mecelle’s maxims
  • 19. No harm, and no answering harm with harm.
  • 20. Harm is to be removed.
  • 30. Repelling a mischief comes before gaining a benefit.
  • 87–88. Burdens go with benefits: the one who gains bears the cost.
  • 92. A direct actor is liable even without intent.
  • 93. One who only causes the loss is liable only if he intended it.
The word planned supplies the intent that art. 93 asks for.

Principle 2 · Harm

Where the principle stops, and the best objection

Direct actor or cause?
  • A2 (an update changes the owner’s device): the maker is the direct actor. Liable even without intent (art. 92).
  • A1, B, C (a design, a withdrawal, a closed repair path): the maker is the cause. Liable if intended (art. 93), and planned obsolescence is by definition intended.
Boundaries
  • The harm must be an actual shortfall against a reasonable expectation, not a lost chance of profit.
  • Natural aging is not harm done by anyone. Only the part caused by the maker’s choice counts.
Objection

“All products lose value over time. Where is the harm?”

Reply

The claim is not that products age. It is that a choice shortened the life, and the test of connection separates the two: without the choice, the owner keeps the product. Aging that no one chose passes the test and is outside the claim; a firmware limit or a sealed part does not pass it.

Principle 2 · Harm

Harm to many: small losses add up

Imagine one baker shorting one customer by one coin. Now imagine the practice built into a million units of a product. The harm to each buyer is the same; the harm to the public is a million times the act.
The weighing, with the Mecelle’s own maxims
  1. If the practice is forbidden, the maker loses the extra sales that early failure would have brought.
  2. If it is allowed, buyers lose money already paid, and the public bears the waste and the repeated spending.
  3. A private harm is accepted to repel a public one. (art. 26)
  4. The heavier harm is removed by the lighter. (art. 27)
  5. So the maker’s lost extra sales yield to the public’s loss.
Two checks
  • Is the maker’s loss even a harm? It is income he has not yet earned, from a sale that works only if the buyer is not told. A profit that exists only through concealment is not a protected interest (Qur’an 4:29).
  • Who gains, who pays? Burdens go with benefits (art. 87–88). The maker takes the benefit, so he bears the cost of telling.
  • Even if his loss counted, art. 31 asks that harm be removed as far as possible: disclosure removes the buyers’ harm and still lets him sell.

STRONG The maxims are explicit; applying them to a firm against a public is by analogy.

Mecelle 26, 27, 31, 87, 88: Berki/Balcı text, read in full.

Principle 3 · Ownership

To own is to be able to use and to maintain

Imagine you buy a car, but the dealer keeps a switch that stops it unless he approves each repair. Did you buy a car, or the right to ask for one?
The norm
N3. A buyer who has paid the price owns the thing and alone decides how it is used, repaired and disposed of.
Why you already hold it
  • If the seller keeps the decisions, he never sold: he leased, and should have called it a lease and priced it as one.
  • If a thing you paid for can be stopped, slowed or locked by someone else at will, then “yours” means nothing in practice.
  • The word sale promises more than a license to use.

Principle 3 · Ownership

Proof: remote control of a sold thing needs the owner’s permission

  1. A sale transfers ownership of the thing and of its benefit to the buyer. (what “full ownership” means in the fiqh)
  2. The owner may use, alter, repair, consume and dispose of what he owns as he wishes, short of harming others. (Mecelle 1192, 1197)
  3. No one may act in another’s property without his permission, nor take it without a lawful ground. (Mecelle 95–97)
  4. What the seller has completed on his side he may not undo. (Mecelle 100)
  5. Slowing, disabling or locking a sold device, or closing its repair, is acting in the owner’s property or undoing a completed sale.
  6. Therefore it is forbidden unless the owner gave a real, informed permission. STRONG

The text of 3.4 is Mecelle 100: whoever tries to undo what is completed on his side, his effort is rejected.

Principle 3 · Ownership

The contract defense, and what the schools say about conditions

The defender’s best text

Muslims are bound by their conditions, except a condition that makes the lawful unlawful or the unlawful lawful. Sunan Abī Dāwūd 3594; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 1352 (ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The text is real, and it binds. The question is what it covers: a condition the buyer knew and freely accepted. A hidden term is not a condition he accepted.

The same texts, the stricter reading

Whoever stipulates a condition that is not in God’s Book, it is void. Sahih al-Bukhārī 2155 (Barīra)

The schools on restrictive conditions
SchoolView, in short
ḤanafīClasses conditions as valid, defective or void. A condition that the buyer may not resell is void; the sale stands.
ShāfiʿīLeans to restricting conditions in contracts.
Mālikī, ḤanbalīLean to freedom: conditions stand unless something forbids them (Ibn Taymiyya: freedom is the rule).

Principle 3 · Ownership

Where the principle stops, and the best objection

Boundaries
  • The owner’s rights end where they harm others (Mecelle 1197, per TDV). He may not use a device to hurt a neighbor.
  • An owner may choose a restriction: automatic security updates, a repair partner, a lease. The ruling concerns restrictions he did not know and did not choose.
  • Where the maker really keeps ownership, he may keep control. Then the price must be a rent and the word must be lease (ijāra).
Objection

“Software is licensed, not sold. The device is only the carrier.”

Reply

In a contract, meaning counts, not wording (Mecelle 3). If the buyer pays a price for permanent possession of a device that cannot work without the software, the contract is a sale of a working device, whatever the label says. A label cannot turn a sale into a lease after the money has changed hands.

Principle 4 · Waste

Usable value may not be destroyed: offered as support only

Imagine a shopkeeper who cracks every plate he sold, a month after the sale. Nobody gains from the broken plates except the one who sells new ones.
The norm
N4. Usable value may not be destroyed for no benefit.
What the texts give, and what they do not
  • “Do not spend wastefully. Indeed, the wasteful are brothers of the devils.” Qur’an 17:26–27.
  • The Prophet said that God dislikes for us wasting wealth (iḍāʿat al-māl). Sahih al-Bukhārī 2408 and Sahih Muslim 593. Al-Bukhārī places it under the chapter heading “What is forbidden of wasting wealth.”
  • But the hadith’s verb is dislikes, not forbids (one Muslim narration says forbade), and the classical discussion is mostly about spending one’s own wealth. The jurists differ on whether it is forbidden or disliked.

SUPPORT ONLY We do not rest the ruling on this. The three earlier norms carry the proof. N4 adds weight where the destruction is chosen, serves no one but the destroyer, and is repeated at scale.

Principle 4 · Waste

Waste and the earth: what we claim, and what we do not

The record
  • The Global E-waste Monitor 2024 reports 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, up 82% since 2010, and that only 22.3% was documented as properly collected and recycled. About US$62 billion of recoverable resources went unaccounted for.
  • Among the causes it names: “shorter product life cycles,” “limited repair options,” and “design shortcomings.”
  • The Qur’an: “Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order” (7:56); “Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people’s hands have done” (30:41).
The argument
  1. A product discarded early because of a maker’s choice adds one unit to the waste stream that would not have been added then. (test of connection)
  2. Those who live with that stream bear its cost and never agreed to it.
  3. That is harm to third parties (N2), and wasteful spending by buyers who must buy again.
Not claimed: that planned obsolescence causes any stated share of the 62 million tonnes. The report names several causes and does not measure this one. Only the direction is argued: each avoidable early discard adds to the stream.

STRONG direction · SUPPORT ONLY size: unmeasured. N4 stays support; the weight rides on N2.

UNITAR/ITU, Global E-waste Monitor 2024, press release (page opened). Qur’an 7:56 and 30:41 (Khattab, quran.com, page opened).

Every defense falls into one of five places

Defense 2

“The buyer agreed”: the test of consent

  1. Was the limit disclosed before the sale, in plain words, where an ordinary buyer would see it? Yes: consent.
  2. If not, is it the publicly known norm of this kind of product, so that no ordinary buyer expects more? Yes: consent by custom.
  3. If not, did the buyer later accept it knowingly, specifically, and with a real option to refuse and keep the device as it was? Yes: consent, for what he knew.
  4. If none: no consent. The limit was concealed.
Why each test is fair

a follows the grain-heap rule: what a buyer cares to see must be placed where he can see it (Muslim 102).

b follows Mecelle 41–45: custom binds when it is settled, prevailing and known.

c follows N1: consent is to a known thing, freely given.

The burden of showing a, b or c lies on the maker, who holds the facts; the buyer need only show the shortfall. (compare Mecelle 76: the proof is on the claimant, and here the maker claims consent)

The maker’s own case

T3: the new product is either better or it is not

Case 1 · the new product is better

Then it gives buyers a reason to buy it. The old device’s early death is not needed to sell it. The “invention” stands on its own merit, and a sound old device is only the platform it improves on.

Case 2 · it is not better

Then the buyer pays again for the same thing. The revenue funds no advance; it funds a repackaging. That is cost to the buyer with no benefit to anyone, which is research in name only.

Either way the defense fails. In case 1 it is unnecessary; in case 2 it is empty.
  • The claimed link is asserted, not shown. Nothing in the idea of a new model requires the old one to fail. The step from “obsolete the old” to “fund the new” is an empirical claim, and the burden of proof is on the one who claims (art. 76).
  • What this assumes: in case 1, that demand responds to real improvement. That is plausible, not certain. If sales were lower, T1 and T2 still apply: price openly instead of hiding.

T4 and T5: it proves too much, and the payer is not the gainer

T4 · Where does the argument stop?
  • If a good end licenses hidden shortfall, a baker may thin his flour to fund a better oven. A builder may skimp the concrete to fund research.
  • Any unagreed charge could be justified this way, by naming a worthy use for the money.
  • But taking another’s wealth without lawful cause is not allowed (art. 97; Qur’an 4:29). The worth of the use does not change how it was taken.

  • Burdens go with benefits (art. 87–88).
  • The buyer pays the hidden cost, then pays again for the new model. The maker gets the funding and the new sale.
  • If research benefits all, all should fund it openly. It cannot be charged to one class of buyer by concealment.
Verdict on the maker’s case. Defense 5 and the funding claim fail T1 to T5. What survives is narrower: a maker may charge openly for research, may end support with notice, and may sell short-lived products honestly described.

The ruling

What the maker must do, may do, and may not do

Must
  • Tell the buyer the designed or rated life, and the support period, where he can see it.
  • Name what an update does before it touches the owner’s device, when it reduces a function.
  • Offer a real choice when a protective measure costs performance.
  • Announce the end of support in good time.
May
  • Sell short-lived goods, if they are honestly described.
  • Sell budget models and premium models side by side.
  • End support with notice; leave devices working.
  • Lease, if it is called a lease and priced as one.
  • Install improving updates under a general advance consent the owner gave and can withdraw.
May not
  • Hide a limit he knows of.
  • Slow, disable or lock a sold device without informed consent.
  • Switch off a service on which a working device depends and which was promised for its lifetime.
  • Reserve the means of repair so that the owner cannot fix what he owns, beyond a narrow stated safety need.

Nothing here requires a product to last long, to be repairable by everyone, or to be supported forever. It requires only that the buyer know and the owner remain in charge.

Case · A1

Phoebus, 1925: a bulb life agreed between makers

The record
  • Phœbus S.A. was incorporated in Geneva on 15 January 1925. Members included Osram, Philips, Tungsram, Associated Electrical Industries and General Electric (through its overseas companies).
  • It set the life of a household bulb at 1,000 hours. Sources give the earlier norm as 1,500–2,000 or 2,500 hours.
  • Members sent samples for testing and paid fines when their bulbs lasted longer than the standard.
  • Krajewski’s reconstruction reports the average life of a reference bulb falling from about 1,800 hours (1926) to about 1,205 hours (1933–34).
  • The cartel ended around 1939. The UK Monopolies Commission (1951) rejected the charge of planned obsolescence, saying there is “no absolutely right life”; engineers note that a hotter filament gives more light and burns out sooner.
Through the tree
  1. A choice of the makers. Yes.
  2. Acts on the product itself, before sale. A1.
  3. Harm? Yes, if buyers expected longer life.
  4. Disclosed? Not established in the sources.
  5. Forced? The light-and-life trade-off is a real necessity if disclosed.
Verdict. The ruling does not depend on motive. It asks whether buyers were told and whether the trade-off was real. A cartel adds a second wrong, since it removes the alternative that might have given buyers a better product.

The doubt court · 1 of 7

Doubts about the claim’s scope

1 · “Doesn’t this forbid cheap, short-lived products?” REJECTED
No. The ruling forbids hiding the limit, not having one. A short life, stated, is a lawful product, and a cheap one honestly described is a good choice for some buyers.
2 · “Who decides what a buyer ordinarily expects?” ACCEPTED IN PART
The custom of that kind of product among buyers and merchants (Mecelle 36–45), together with the maker’s own statements and advertising. Where custom is unclear, the maker ends the dispute by stating the life.
3 · “A maker cannot know a product’s exact life.” ACCEPTED IN PART
He need not state what he cannot know. He must state what he does know: rated cycles, design hours, planned support dates. Ignorance of the unknowable excuses; silence about the known does not.
4 · “Durability is just a feature the market can price.” REJECTED
Only a disclosed feature can be priced. A buyer cannot put a price on what he cannot see, and the market cannot punish a limit it does not know of.
Verdict on the doubt: REJECTED the doubt fails · ACCEPTED IN PART it holds within the limits the answer states · ACCEPTED it is right, and consistent with the ruling · OUT OF SCOPE not what the claim covers.

Doubts about proof and intent

9 · “You cannot prove the maker’s intent.” REJECTED
The ruling attaches to what can be shown: that he knew the limit (tests, design documents, rated lives), that he did not disclose it, and that the buyer lost. Inner motive is not needed. The burden of showing consent lies on the maker, who claims it (Mecelle 76).

A market can punish only what buyers can see. A hidden limit is the one thing they cannot see. The market is blind exactly where the ruling is needed.

It matters for blame and punishment (Mecelle 92–93; taʿzīr). It does not matter for whether the buyer must be told: that duty binds whoever knows.

The proof, summed up

The whole argument in one minute

  1. A price pays for the life a buyer expects.
  2. A maker who knows the product will give less, and says nothing, takes part of the price without consent. That is deceit, and the classical texts forbid it. (N1)
  3. The same choice harms the buyer and profits the maker, with no right behind it. (N2)
  4. What was sold is owned. Slowing, disabling or locking it from afar acts on another’s property. (N3)
  5. Only five defenses exist: no harm, consent, necessity, entitlement, others’ benefit. Each fails, or only narrows the claim. The strongest, “it funds innovation,” fails five tests: it is hidden, a lawful route exists, the new product is either better or empty, it proves too much, and the payer is not the gainer.
  6. What stays lawful: an honest short life, support ended with notice, leases, restrictions the owner chose.
  7. So: tell the buyer, ask the owner, and leave him in charge.

The proof, summed up

The price of rejecting the ruling

To reject the ruling you must……and then accept that
Deny that a buyer pays for what he expects (step 1.1).A price carries no information. No one could rationally pay in advance for anything.
Deny that concealment removes consent (N1).Fraud in any sale is consensual, since every victim of fraud “agreed.”
Deny that this is harm (N2).A seller may shift losses onto buyers whenever it pays him, if they are not told.
Say the sold device stays the maker’s (N3).Then it was a lease. The price was rent, the word “sale” was false, and the buyer should have been told.
Say the facts are absent in a given case (F1–F3).Then the case is outside the claim, and the claim agrees with you. You must show it.
Say the maker needs the money for research.Then he may say so and price openly (T1–T2). A model that is better needs no hidden failure; one that is not better funds nothing (T3).
Accept a defense (D1–D5).The answer already given to it, on the defense’s own slide.

So the ruling is not shielded by authority. It is open to anyone who can name a premise to give up, and the cost is shown beside each one.

The ruling in force

What is forbidden, what is required, what is permitted

Forbidden. To hide a limit on a product’s life or repair that the maker knows and the buyer does not. To slow, disable or lock a sold device, or to switch off a service it was promised, without the owner’s informed consent. To close the owner’s own means of repair beyond a narrow, stated safety need.

Required. To tell the buyer the designed life and the support period where he can see them. To name what an update does when it costs a function, and to offer a real choice. To announce the end of support in good time and leave the device working.

Permitted. Short-lived products honestly described. Budget and premium models together. Ending support with notice. Leases called leases. Updates under a general consent the owner can withdraw.

Remedy. Rescission or compensation for the buyer, restoration where the device was changed, and taʿzīr by the ruler.

Limits of the claim

What this talk does not claim

  • That every short-lived product is wrongful. A short life that the buyer knows of is a lawful product.
  • That every maker intends harm. The ruling concerns the act: a known limit, hidden or imposed. Motive affects blame, not the duty to tell.
  • That products must be repairable by everyone, or supported forever. The ruling asks for notice, honesty and leaving the owner in charge.
  • That updates are forbidden. Updates are lawful under informed consent; only those that reduce a function need a specific one.
  • That a ban would raise or lower the rate of innovation, or that research budgets would grow by any amount. Those are hypotheses H1 to H3; the proof does not depend on them.
  • That planned obsolescence causes any stated share of the world’s e-waste. The size is unmeasured; only the direction is argued.
  • That fashion, advertising or taste are wrong. The claim concerns the device and the means of keeping it working.
  • That the four public cases prove the ruling. They test the map; they illustrate the tree. The ruling stands on the principles.
  • That any company is guilty today. Court and regulator records are cited as records, with their limits.
